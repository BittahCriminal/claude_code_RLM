Introducing cloud-native security
As we’ve seen in the previous
chapters, the cloud offers significant advantages in accelerating solution development and deployment while optimizing costs. However, these benefits can only be fully realized if we also modernize how we approach security. Cloud-native security embraces the shift-left principle, which emphasizes incorporating security earlier in the asset life cycle. Addressing security from the outset helps avoid late-stage issues and deployment delays. That said, implementing this approach is often more challenging than it sounds.
In many cases, developers—particularly early adopters—and infrastructure teams have adopted modern practices such as infrastructure as code, while security teams remain entrenched in traditional, reactive, and
waterfall-driven processes. It’s not uncommon for firewall rule changes to take days to be approved and implemented, which stands in stark contrast to the shift-left philosophy and undermines the progress made by other teams.
With the right tools and technologies, security has become declarative. As discussed in
Chapter 3
,
Infrastructure Design
, and
Chapter 4
,
Working with Azure Kubernetes Service (AKS)
, CI/CD pipelines enable organizations to provision both code and infrastructure consistently. The same automation can be extended to security, especially in cloud-native platforms such as Kubernetes, where elements such as layer 4 (transport) and layer 7 (application) policies are defined as code and automated.
However, cloud-native security is not just about tools and technology—it requires a cultural shift. Organizations must rethink their structure and embrace a new mindset, which can be difficult in environments with deep-rooted legacy practices. Regardless of your industry or regulatory landscape, there are always opportunities to improve automation and streamline security processes. Regulations typically define
what
must be achieved, not
how
to achieve it. Many organizations in the insurance and financial industries entirely rely on manual processes and lengthy approval steps before opening a flow between a source and a target. While this is understandable, it dramatically impacts the time to market and agility.
In a cloud-native model, security architects must create value by enabling automation and ensuring that security processes do not hinder progress. They should serve as
enablers
—educating other teams on security’s importance, offering practical solutions, and supporting risk-based decisions. They must assess risks objectively and allow the business to determine which residual risks are acceptable. They should contribute to architectural patterns that encompass the security dimension so as to quicken approval processes.
This mindset shift is particularly difficult in risk-averse sectors such as banking, where cautiousness is ingrained. The degree to which an organization can evolve from traditional to cloud-native security practices often depends on its inherent culture. But one thing is clear: while cloud technologies can deliver on promises of cost efficiency, speed, resilience, and scalability, these outcomes are
unreachable
without a fundamental change in security practices. Security is a core pillar of cloud success—or failure.
From a technical standpoint, there has also been a significant shift toward identity as the primary layer of defense rather than the network, although Microsoft has closed many gaps in terms of network capabilities over
recent years. However, network-based security still tends to be more complex, costly, and rigid. Unfortunately, many traditional security architects continue to rely heavily on perimeter-focused strategies (the DMZ mindset), often overlooking identity-based security.
In this chapter, we will offer guidance on adopting a cloud-native security posture and highlight traditional approaches that may hinder your cloud journey. Keep in mind that your ability to succeed in the cloud is closely tied to your ability to modernize your security practices.
Let’s explore some key guidelines to help establish the right mindset and lay strong foundations for your cloud security journey.
Laying the foundations for cloud security
After years of working extensively with Azure, I’ve observed a recurring pattern across many organizations: security teams
often replicate their on-premises security stack in the cloud without adaptation. Familiar names such as F5, Netskope, Palo Alto firewalls, Splunk, QRadar, and CyberArk frequently make their way into cloud environments
by default
. While these established tools are not inherently problematic, they often introduce additional complexity and are adopted without evaluating Microsoft-native alternatives.
The rationale is usually the same: the desire for a unified
single pane of glass
across environments, or statements such as: “
Palo Alto firewalls are better than Azure Firewall
.” While it is true that niche products are often richer than Azure alternatives, too often, decisions are driven by product familiarity and internal expertise rather than actual business, regulatory, or technical requirements. A key contributor to this trend is the lack of Azure/Microsoft-specific expertise within security teams. How many certified Azure security professionals can you typically find in a CISO organization? Think about your own organization and you will be surprised by the number. It’s often close to zero.
The single-pane-of-glass argument is very valid and used to justify relying on legacy or third-party security tools across environments. However, it’s important to recognize that many Azure-native services are now
designed to work beyond Azure, supporting hybrid and multi-cloud scenarios as well. Microsoft has gradually renamed several services to emphasize that they are no longer limited to Azure. As for concerns about feature gaps between Azure-native products and third-party solutions, Microsoft has consistently shown a strong pace of innovation, rapidly closing gaps, and introducing new capabilities. Once a native service gains traction, feature parity with more established competitors is often reached sooner than expected.
The added complexity of using non-native products stems from the operational overhead they introduce. When relying on third-party tools, organizations are fully responsible for ensuring high availability, scalability, and disaster recovery—capabilities that are typically built into Azure services natively and managed by Microsoft. Additionally, native solutions offer tighter and better integration, simplified management, and higher resilience, allowing teams to focus more on outcomes rather than infrastructure.
Here are a few principles that might help trigger the correct behavior when designing security solutions in Azure and Kubernetes:
Know the risk appetite of your organization
: Security decisions should primarily be guided by the organization’s risk appetite and business drivers. There is no one-size-fits-all approach—what works for a bank with limited risk tolerance will differ significantly from a digital-native company offering entertainment services.
Best of suite over best of breed
: Whatever security challenge you’re addressing, always start by evaluating the native Azure offering and how well it integrates with the broader Azure ecosystem. For example, most Azure services are designed to work seamlessly with Azure Key Vault for managing secrets, certificates, and encryption keys. Choosing a third-party vault solution could introduce friction or limit this native integration. Furthermore, all Azure services support full automation through infrastructure as code. If you opt for custom or third-party solutions, you must ensure they align with these automation capabilities and evaluate how they integrate
with the Azure ecosystem, to avoid disrupting your cloud experience. Following this principle is even more important in container orchestration platforms.
Assess trust in Microsoft and clearly identify your responsibilities
: The cloud operates on a shared responsibility model, which inherently means relinquishing full control over certain aspects of the infrastructure. While this may seem straightforward, many security teams struggle to fully embrace this reality. Rather than attempting to uncover the inner workings of multi-tenant service offerings, organizations should focus on clearly understanding and addressing their own areas of responsibility within the model. This shift in mindset is essential for building an effective and sustainable cloud security posture. The other aspects that you do not control should be covered by SLAs and Microsoft’s liability with regard to security breaches that may affect your organization. It is then up to your legal department to evaluate this part.
Conduct thorough risk assessments and document residual risks
: Many security teams still lack robust risk assessment practices. A common example is the handling of container image vulnerabilities. It’s not unusual to see blanket policies—such as
zero high vulnerabilities
—that block
deployments without evaluating the actual risk or context. This rigid approach often leads to unnecessary friction and delays. A more effective strategy involves assessing whether identified vulnerabilities are exploitable within your specific environment, considering other mitigating controls already in place. In some cases, it may be entirely acceptable to deploy containers with high-severity vulnerabilities if they pose no real threat under current conditions. This observation applies to everything you do in Azure, not just containers.
Adapting CISO practices to cloud-native realities
: Shift left and DevSecOps are far more than industry buzzwords—they are critical enablers for adopting cloud-native practices and unlocking the full potential of the cloud. However, their effectiveness is limited if CISO-led security processes are not reimagined to align with the realities of modern, inherently disruptive IT environments. In a world where everything is defined as code, developers and infrastructure engineers can now implement network and service mesh policies directly. While these actions must remain under the
CISO’s oversight, they are incompatible with traditional ticket-based workflows. This highlights the urgent need for the CISO function to evolve and embrace new operational models that support agility without compromising compliance.
Choose appropriate compliance frameworks
: When selecting a security framework, choose one that aligns well with cloud environments and with local regulatory obligations. For instance, ISO/IEC 27001:2013 does not fully account for the shared responsibility model, as it includes controls related to physical infrastructure—something cloud customers typically cannot influence. If you choose ISO, consider adopting the 2022 revision, which better reflects modern IT realities. Alternatively, frameworks such as CIS Controls v8.1 offer more actionable guidance for assessing cloud-specific hardening and SecOps. Remember, frameworks are not mutually exclusive—you can combine them to cover different aspects of your security posture. Make sure to stay away from any in-house compliance frameworks as they will likely not be relevant at all for a cloud environment.

Understanding encryption mechanisms and capabilities
Before diving into the specific
details, let’s first review the different encryption types and Azure-related capabilities:
Encryption at rest
: This type of
encryption safeguards data at rest (stored) by ensuring it remains unreadable if the
storage device is stolen or accessed with malicious intent. Most Azure services let you
Bring Your Own Key
(
BYOK
) or, by default, use Microsoft-managed keys to ensure data at rest is always
encrypted. An additional
possibility is
Host Your Own Key
(
HYOK
), which
means that you generate the keys and store them in hardware that you control.
Be aware that this option is mostly compatible with Infrastructure as a Service
.
Encryption in transit
: This type of encryption protects data in motion. For example, when accessing
https://portal.azure.com
using a web browser, the client initiates a TLS connection
with the Azure server. During this process, the client verifies the legitimacy of the server by validating its certificate, and both sides perform a key exchange to derive a shared symmetric encryption key. This key is then used to encrypt all subsequent data transmitted between the client and server.
Asymmetric
cryptography is used during the handshake to authenticate the server and secure the key exchange process. Simply put, this type of encryption ensures that someone sniffing the network would only see encrypted data and not clear text. As for encryption at rest, Azure services default to Microsoft-managed certificates, but most services let you
Bring Your Own Certificate
(
BYOC
). When
using BYOC, you typically store them in Azure Key Vault and integrate Azure services with Key Vault to let them pull the
certificate. You may
Host Your Own Certificate
(
HYOC
) by storing private keys in hardware that you control.
Be aware that this is absolutely not compatible with Azure PaaS services
.
Encryption in use
: This type of
encryption protects in-memory data. As with the previous example, without encryption in use, any administrator or system process of a machine might perform memory inspection and see data in clear text. In Azure, encryption in
use is offered through the broader
confidential computing
concept.
Encryption at rest and in transit in Azure rely by default on Microsoft-managed keys. This is by far the easiest way to leverage Azure services. Many companies rely on these defaults and only bring their own certificates for internet-facing web apps and APIs. From that perspective, it’s worth noting that Azure Key Vault has built-in integration mechanisms with well-known
Certificate Authorities
(
CAs
), such as GlobalSign
and DigiCert, including automatic certificate renewal.
However, many other companies have regulatory obligations to store encryption keys in HSMs or do not feel comfortable with the US Patriot Act, allowing the
National Security Agency
(
NSA
) to request data from any US company, wherever the data is stored, whether on national territory or anywhere else. Of course, companies such as Microsoft and Amazon resist these legal obligations, but the risk is
definitely not null. It is worth mentioning that this statement does not apply to Azure China, since Microsoft doesn’t operate those data centers.
For this very reason, many organizations turn to customer-managed keys to strengthen their data protection. This naturally raises the question: where should these keys be stored? How can we be safe against the previously mentioned threats?
The safest approach to mitigate all the risks linked to potential third-party interference is HYOK, where encryption keys never leave your on-premises infrastructure. Unfortunately, this approach is compatible with a very limited number of services, such as Microsoft 365. Additionally, keeping keys on-premises introduces latency and can undermine resilience by creating a tight dependency between the cloud and your on-premises infrastructure—an approach that is far from ideal.
HYOK aside, Azure comes with many different options to store your keys. The full list, along with the drivers that should help you choose the best option for your use case, is available here:
https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/security/fundamentals/key-management-choose
.
There are a few factors to consider before choosing an option:
Service model
: What service model are you primarily using—IaaS, PaaS, CaaS, or FaaS? This question alone can rule out several options. For instance, Azure Dedicated HSM doesn’t natively integrate with any Azure PaaS services, making it incompatible with that model. On the other hand, a few PaaS services support Azure Managed HSM, and nearly all of them are compatible with Azure Key Vault.
Regulatory requirements
: Regulatory requirements are often tied to
Federal Information Processing Standards
(
FIPS
) levels. Select the option that aligns most closely with your compliance obligations.
Root of trust control and key sovereignty
: Choose any of the compatible options if you require full
control over your encryption keys and want to ensure that Microsoft has no access to them.

Now, let’s dive a bit deeper into the underlying encryption mechanisms.
Understanding data encryption keys and key encryption keys
Something important to grasp is the role
of the
Data Encryption Key
(
DEK
) and
Key Encryption Key
(
KEK
). When you BYOK, you actually bring your own KEK. This KEK never leaves the boundaries
of the HSM. Asymmetric encryption is used to encrypt the DEK through key wrap and unwrap operations. The
encrypted DEK is stored on the server side in
the Azure service, but the unencrypted symmetric key lives in memory. This means that the actual encryption key used to encrypt the data is in fact available in the memory of the servers used by the service. One way to protect in-memory information is to rely on encryption in use (discussed in the next section), which translates to confidential computing in Azure. While you can use confidential computing for virtual machines and container solutions such as AKS, you do not control this for PaaS services. One way to retain control over the DEKs is through client-side encryption, where data is encrypted before being stored in the cloud. However, this approach can be cumbersome, can introduce performance overhead, and does not play well with cloud-only workloads.
Introducing encryption in use
Data-in-use encryption protects against in-memory attacks such as memory dumps, compromised processes, and
memory scraping. Without such encryption, sensitive data in memory may be exposed in plaintext.
The goal is to encrypt in-memory data and process it inside secure enclaves, which are protected environments that restrict access to authorized code.
Trusted Execution Environments
(
TEEs
), such as Intel SGX and
AMD SEV, enable these enclaves using hardware-based security features.
Confidential computing is a broader paradigm that encompasses TEEs and other technologies to protect data while it is in use—complementing encryption at rest and in transit. Azure includes several services that are associated with confidential computing. You can visit this link to get an exhaustive list:
https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/solutions/confidential-compute
.
Let’s now look at different ways to maintain data integrity.
Preserving data integrity
Data access can be controlled using Azure
Role-Based Access Control
(
RBAC
). All Azure services—whether data-centric or not—generate
activity logs that capture control plane operations. These logs can be ingested by Azure Sentinel or any other SIEM solution for comprehensive monitoring. For data plane activities, certain services, such as Azure SQL, provide dedicated audit logs.
To safeguard against
accidental deletions or modifications, Azure offers features such as soft delete, purge protection, and versioning for some services. Cosmos DB includes a change feed mechanism that tracks operations performed on data. Additionally, Azure SQL and Synapse support dynamic data masking, allowing sensitive information to be obfuscated at runtime. Additionally, backup solutions such as a Backup vault allow us to take immutable backups.
In summary, it’s essential to verify which features are supported by the data services you intend to use and to enable data protection-specific features.

Introducing authentication, authorization, and Conditional Access policies
The terms authentication
and authorization are often confused—partly because certain features straddle both domains.
Authentication is the process
whereby a user submits credentials to an identity
provider, which then verifies them. Once successfully validated, the user is signed in.
Authorization determines whether a user or application has permission to access a particular resource. It’s the responsibility of the resource server to allow or deny access.
This distinction can become blurred with features such as
Conditional Access
, which intersects both processes.
For instance, during user sign-in, Conditional Access might prompt for
Multi-Factor Authentication
(
MFA
) based on specific
conditions (such as login attempts from unfamiliar locations), but it can also serve as an access gatekeeper—deciding, for example, whether a user can enter the Azure portal depending on preconfigured rules. Blocking access is more part of the authorization realm than the authentication one. Conditional Access can be seen as the policy engine of a zero-trust security model that makes decisions based on signals received from devices, users, networks, and locations.
All of this can contribute to confusion between authentication and authorization.
The
Identity Protection
feature protects against malicious authentication activities.
Figure 9.7
illustrates the different features made available by
Identity Protection

Figure 9.7 – Identity Protection
Identity Protection is mostly useful for detecting three types of risks: user, sign-in, and workload identity risks.
A user risk means that the user might have been compromised. A sign-in risk is identified while the user signs in. A workload identity risk means that a service principal (non-human user) might have been compromised.
By combining risk detection
with CAPs, we can prevent potentially compromised users or
applications from accessing specific resources. CAPs allow
us to define powerful rules. For example, very common rules that can be defined are the following:
Triggering MFA when accessing specific resources
Location-based policies, such as either requiring MFA or denying/granting access based on the user’s location
Requiring full re-authentication for risk-based policies
Enforcing the use of corporate devices only
Triggering MFA if a low/medium sign-in risk is identified
Blocking access in case a high user, sign-in, or workload identity risk is identified
All these policies typically end up with either blocking or granting access, or triggering MFA.

Another possibility is to have a non-production tenant (a separate instance of Entra ID) where you can test those policies.
An additional
authentication-related
feature is
Smart Lockout
, which helps prevent brute-force attacks by only locking the
account after a configurable number of
failed attempts, and by imposing a configurable time interval across login attempts.
Lastly, Entra ID has a few passwordless options and integrates seamlessly with FIDO2/passkeys, the Microsoft
Authenticator app, as well as
Temporary Access Pass
(
TAP
) and QR code-based authentication. TAP allows you to register passwordless authentication methods.
In terms of authorization, Entra ID has directory-specific roles, such as Application Developer, Application Administrator, and Global Administrator, which all allow you to manage and configure Entra ID. For any Azure-related resources, we rely on Azure RBAC, which features hundreds of roles. It is important to consider the Azure hierarchy that we described in
Chapter 2
,
Solution Architecture
, since it is where we apply RBAC permissions and policies. We’ll tackle other authorization-specific aspects in the next section.
Introducing common protocols such as OIDC, OAuth 2.0, and SAML
SAML is a widely
recognized legacy protocol
that remains in active use today, particularly in federation scenarios. However, cloud-native
applications typically rely on OAuth 2.0 and
OpenID Connect
(
OIDC

adopting OIDC. As such, our focus will be exclusively on OAuth 2.0 and OIDC. That said, it’s important to acknowledge that SAML continues to play a role and is expected to persist for the foreseeable future.
Both OAuth 2.0 and OIDC
are international standards and are not specific to Azure. In this
section, we will examine how Entra ID supports these protocols, noting that its implementation does not fully align with the specifications in all aspects.

Understanding OIDC endpoints and mapping them to Entra ID
Any OIDC interaction
must go through one of the endpoints

The
Discovery

The

Understanding token types, lifetime, revocation, and Entra ID limitations
We already slightly introduced token types in
Chapter 6
,
Developing and Designing Applications with Azure
, but we’ll provide a broader coverage in this section. Let’s start by exploring the different token types.
Figure 9.13
illustrates the different token types that exist in OIDC:
Figure 9.13 – Token types
Here is an explanation of the four token types:
ID tokens
: These contain
user-related information. They are mostly used in client applications, such
as
Single-Page Applications
(
SPAs
) or mobile apps. ID tokens typically
contain claims such as
preferred_name
or
email
. They may also contain a pointer to the groups a given user belongs to. ID tokens should never be used outside of the business application’s client interface. In other words, they are not meant to be used for API or resource access.
Access tokens
: These represent
a credential issued by an authorization server (for example, Entra ID) that allows the client application to access resources, such as
custom and third-party APIs, on behalf of the logged-in user or as an application. With Entra ID,
access tokens are not opaque but self-contained value tokens
. This means that any entity in possession of a valid access token can access the resource for which it was issued, regardless of how the token was obtained. As an analogy, an access token is like a set of house keys: you don’t need to be the owner to enter—if you have the keys and know where the house is, you can get inside. Access tokens
cannot
be revoked in Entra ID.
It is therefore important to consider their lifetime. It’s worth noting that Entra ID
supports
Continuous Access Evaluation
(
CAE
) based on the OpenID
Continuous Access Evaluation Profile
(
CAEP
), allowing your applications
and APIs to respond to critical events such as password changes or account disablement—provided they are CAE-aware themselves. More on this here:
https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity-platform/app-resilience-continuous-access-evaluation
.
Refresh tokens
: These are
long-lived credentials used to obtain new access tokens on behalf of the authenticated user. They play a key role in avoiding repeated
user authentication when access tokens expire, which is especially beneficial for mobile applications and browser-based clients. However, due to their extended lifespan and critical role in maintaining session continuity, exposing refresh tokens in public clients poses significant security risks. A
recommended best practice is to implement the
Backend for Frontend
(
BFF
) pattern (described in the next section), which ensures that all token handling remains on the server side, preventing any type of token from being exposed to the client. A few Entra ID events (changing password, user disabled, etc.) may revoke existing refresh tokens depending on whether they are used by confidential or public clients. Administrators can, however, enforce a refresh token revocation for a given user, which applies to all client types.
Reference tokens
: These are opaque access tokens and they are
not supported
in Entra ID. They act as a pointer to the authorization server. The benefit of using such tokens is that they
can be revoked at any point in time. The APIs receiving such tokens cannot validate them directly and must call the authorization
server’s introspection endpoint. This type of endpoint doesn’t exist in Entra ID. Be aware that even if Entra ID did support them, using this type of token would introduce a performance penalty and would require a highly scalable authorization server, since it would have to handle many more requests. Alternatives to Entra ID, such as Duende and Keycloak, support reference tokens. However, they might be more useful in CIAM scenarios, where you may use Entra External ID for authentication and Duende or Keycloak for authorization.
It is worth noting that the token lifetime also depends on the scenario and the identity that is used to request a token. Here are the different types of identities available in Entra ID:
Registered applications
: This type of object is explicitly registered, either to expose and secure a custom
API or to enable API access for client applications. The registration process depends on whether the application is intended for use by a public or a confidential client. When assigning permissions, we differentiate between
delegated
and
application
permissions. Delegated permissions allow the client application to access a resource (for example, the Graph API) on behalf of the signed-in user, provided that the user or an administrator has granted consent. This type of app often requires a client secret or a client certificate, depending on the chosen authorization flow. Be aware that using a client certificate is considered more secure but adds some extra constraints for the business application using the client app. The token lifetime is one hour by default but can be modified through specific policies. This type of application also benefits from enterprise features such as the possibility to restrict its use to specific users and groups and map group memberships to custom API roles.
Managed identities
: This type of object is provisioned by Azure. In practice, we create Azure resources that are given either system-assigned or user-assigned managed identities, which
automatically results in the creation of a corresponding service principal in Entra ID. When these managed identities are used to obtain access tokens, the tokens have a fixed lifetime of 24 hours. This duration cannot be altered. The reason is that applications using managed identities do not communicate directly with Entra ID’s token endpoint. Instead, they interact with a local endpoint provided by Azure on the Azure service (for example, app service, virtual machine, and so on). This local endpoint caches tokens for 24 hours, and there is no supported policy mechanism to override this behavior. Note that the use of managed identities is mostly restricted to accessing Azure resources such as an Azure SQL database or a Service Bus instance. It is technically possible to use managed identities to consume custom APIs, but it is not so common in practice.
Workload identity federation through user-assigned identities
: This type of identity allows you to
establish trust between an external system, such as AKS or GitHub, and Entra ID. The external system becomes a token issuer whose tokens can be exchanged with Entra ID tokens through federated identities materialized using user-assigned managed identities. As a consequence, the token lifetime is also 24 hours. As for managed identities, we typically use federation through user-assigned identities to access Azure resources and leverage the Azure RBAC model to grant permissions.
Workload identity federation through app registration
: This type of identity allows you to establish trust between
an external system and Entra ID, but the way they integrate is through an app registration. As a consequence, the token lifetime defaults to one hour and can be adjusted using custom policies.
While leveraging both managed identities and workload identities is considered a best practice, the fixed lifetime of the resulting access tokens can raise concerns. Nevertheless, these types of identities are widely adopted due to their convenience when accessing Azure-only resources. Infrastructure as code pipelines do not require direct interaction with Entra ID—they only need to provision user-assigned identities, which automatically result in corresponding service principals. In contrast, using app registrations instead of user-assigned identities introduces scalability challenges; they require direct access to Entra ID and support only up to 20 federated credentials per application, but one of the greatest benefits is that they can be used with Conditional Access, unlike the other methods. So, it might be a good option for some specific scenarios.
In Kubernetes environments such as AKS, where hundreds or thousands of Pods may exist, adhering to the least-privilege principle would necessitate a large number of app registrations—an approach that does not scale effectively.
Grasping the different authorization flows and token acquisition mechanisms
It is essential to identify which authorization flow is appropriate for each type of application. Many developers and security
professionals lack a clear understanding of how these flows should be used, which can easily result in security vulnerabilities and the leak of tokens/secrets.
As we highlighted earlier, the notion
of public and confidential clients is important. Public clients are applications that cannot securely store credentials or client secrets. For example, a web browser can store data in its web storage (session or local storage) but the information is easily accessible by anyone with access to the device. In the case of a web browser, malicious JavaScript code can also easily be injected and access that sensitive information. Similarly, mobile apps, user laptops, and so on are considered public clients as their owner/user can typically extract the information. It is, therefore, important not to store a
shared
secret on such devices. An example of a shared secret might be the secret obtained during the app registration process or an API key, or even the root key of a storage account.
Conversely, confidential clients are server-side components that can easily store secrets and that are managed by infrastructure
administrators. For example, a custom backend service could store a secret in Azure
Key Vault and pull it at runtime using its managed identity. The secret cannot be seen directly by the end user.
The type of authorization flow we can use depends on the type of client used by the business application. Technically, there is nothing preventing the use of a confidential client on an end user device.
For example, you could embed a client ID and client secret in a mobile app package and use it from a mobile device. However, such credentials could easily be extracted through package inspection or traffic capture. It is, therefore, the responsibility of security analysts and architects to assess and mitigate the associated risks.
Figure 9.14
illustrates the different flows available in OIDC:
Figure 9.14 – Authorization flows
Public clients, such as SPAs, historically
relied on the
Implicit Grant
flow. However, due to
its security vulnerabilities, this flow is now deprecated and should be avoided. It may still be encountered in legacy applications. The recommended alternative for public clients is the
Proof Key for Code Exchange
(
PKCE
) flow, which
offers stronger protection by introducing a dynamically generated secret to mitigate interception attacks. PKCE is used together with a secretless Authorization Code flow.
For
Model-View-Controller
(
MVC
) applications, which are considered confidential clients, several authorization flows are
available. The
Authorization Code
flow relies on a pair of
credentials (client ID and client secret) that can be securely stored on the server side and, as its name indicates, returns an authorization
code to the client, which can then be exchanged for tokens.
When an ID token is needed during
the initial interaction with the authorization server, one
option is the
Hybrid
flow—a combination of the deprecated Implicit Grant and the Authorization Code flow—or, preferably, the more secure PKCE in combination with the Authorization Code flow.
In short, the PKCE flow is recommended for all clients, whether public or confidential.
For machine-to-machine or service-to-service
interactions, the most commonly used flow is the
Client Credentials
flow. It relies on a pair of credentials—a client ID and a client secret—which is appropriate for confidential clients. The flow is straightforward, involving a single HTTP request to the token endpoint to obtain an access token.
A more secure variant uses a client ID in combination with a client certificate instead of a secret. In this approach, the public key of the certificate must be uploaded to the application registered in Entra ID. The client application then generates a client assertion—a JWT token signed with its private key—to authenticate itself. This method is considered more secure because it leverages asymmetric cryptography: the private key stays with the client, while only the public key is shared with Entra ID, avoiding the risks associated with shared secrets. An even more secure version of the Client Credentials flow is to use
certificate-bound access tokens
, which include certificate information inside the token, but this is not supported by Entra.
An additional machine-to-machine flow is
Resource Owner Password Grant
, which is discouraged but still used in situations
where a resource does not support the Client Credentials flow and the resource consumer does not have a human user. For example, the consumer could be a background job that must interact with a resource with the equivalent of a service account. In that case, the service account is like a user, but the username and password are used by a machine instead of a human.
Lastly, the
Device Code
flow is designed for
devices that do not have a browser or have very limited UI capabilities. The device sends a request to Entra ID, waits for a code to display to the user, and then repeatedly polls the token
endpoint with the received code until the user is authenticated, after which an access
token is returned to the device.
You can easily watch this flow in action by sniffing the traffic of the
az login --use-device-code
command.
Using PKCE for public clients is important, but it’s not the only consideration. You also need to decide whether tokens should be returned directly to the client device. For years, SPAs and mobile apps were typically designed to receive tokens directly from the authorization server. However, in recent years, the BFF pattern—originally intended to serve client-specific needs—has emerged as a preferred approach. It acts as an intermediary, helping to keep tokens off the client device and enhancing security by limiting exposure.
Figure 9.15
illustrates the BFF pattern used for requesting and managing tokens on behalf of client devices:
Figure 9.15 – BFF pattern with OIDC
Be aware that most
application teams push back on this implementation as they see
this as a constraint. A potential trade-off consists of enforcing the BFF pattern for any internet-facing asset and possibly allowing user tokens to surface in public clients for internal and corporate apps. The risks encountered in letting tokens go back to the client are mostly linked to JavaScript-based (cross-site scripting, etc.) and
Man-in-the-Middle
(
MITM
) attacks. Note that while the cookie-based approach protects against JavaScript-based
attacks (providing the cookie is set to
HttpOnly
), it does not protect against MITM.
Scalability is another key consideration
when implementing the BFF pattern. In a traditional setup without a BFF, tokens are
managed and stored locally on each client device, effectively distributing the storage across all users. In contrast, introducing a BFF centralizes token management, requiring server-side storage for all active sessions. This typically necessitates a distributed cache such as Redis, along with proper session cleanup mechanisms. Additionally, placing a BFF between the client and the target resource introduces extra latency. As with any architectural choice, adopting BFF involves trade-offs between security, scalability, and performance.
Next, we’ll see how to perform proper token validation and clarify the role of scopes, which is often misunderstood.
Getting up to speed with token validation and access control mechanisms
Token validation is an important task that
should be correctly understood.
There are two schools of thought: those who believe that relying solely on an API gateway without additional validation in the backend code is sufficient, and those who believe the backend should always validate the token, regardless of the architecture in place. I belong to the latter. I think it is important to always validate the token in the code because the backend represents the resource server. The gateway is useful to stop illegal requests earlier, but you should not rely only on the gateway. Moreover, any change in the architecture could expose the backend.
However, by default, application code does not enforce any token validation. It is the responsibility of developers to implement proper validation logic to ensure the received tokens are correctly verified. In my experience conducting numerous API audits, I have consistently encountered security flaws—such as unprotected API operations or entire controllers lacking the proper access controls. Additionally, there
is often a misunderstanding of what proper token validation looks like.
Figure 9.16
shows the different access control mechanisms that must be validated:
Figure 9.16 – Access control mechanisms
First and foremost, it’s important to understand that the structure and content of an access token can vary depending on the authorization flow used to obtain it. An access token issued on behalf of a user typically includes more claims than one obtained via the Client Credentials flow, which represents an application-only context. Regardless of the flow or token format, certain elements must always be validated systematically:
The signature
: The authorization server signs the token using its private key. To ensure its authenticity, you must validate the signature using the corresponding public key, which can be retrieved from the URL specified in the
jwks_uri
attribute found in the OIDC well-known metadata endpoint. Relying solely on the token’s payload without verifying its signature poses a serious security risk.
The issuer
: The
iss
claim represents the issuer. Checking this ensures the token was generated by a trusted issuer.
The audience
: The
aud
claim represents
the audience(s), meaning the identifier of the resource server(s). With this, you are sure that the client effectively requested a token to talk to you.
The expiration date
: The
exp
claim represents the expiration date and time of the token. Checking this ensures you do not let stale tokens go through.
The not before claim
: The
nbf
claim should ideally be verified as it represents the time from which backends can process it.
It may also be useful to check the
appid
claim, which represents the identifier of the client application used by the business service, if you want to restrict a backend to a given caller or a limited set of callers. This can be particularly useful in server-to-server scenarios.
Beyond the basic validations, roles and scopes can also be leveraged—though they fulfill entirely distinct functions. RBAC is applied by inspecting the role claim, which may include one or more entries representing the roles defined by the resource server. These roles are injected by Entra ID, either statically according to the client application’s registration or through user or group assignments to the resource server’s application object, which links specific user groups to designated roles.
For more advanced scenarios, RBAC might not
be sufficient, and
Attribute-Based Access Control
(
ABAC
) should be used instead. ABAC determines access based on a combination of attributes that reflect business rules. ABAC often requires
Claims Augmentation
to inject additional claims next to the default ones. For example, ABAC might be useful with rules such as this:
Allow access if the user is a French subscriber and has a gold-level subscription
.
Next in line is the
scope
claim, which is often a source of confusion in OAuth 2.0. Many developers tend to rely on scopes without deeper scrutiny—simply verifying their presence in a token to allow API access. However, scopes should be treated as tools to define the boundaries of your APIs. They
enable the authorization server to clearly communicate to users that a client application seeks to access specific features of a resource server on their behalf. This facilitates an informed consent experience, empowering users to either approve or reject the requested access. A user might have consented to the requested access, but it doesn’t mean the user actually has that permission over the resource server. Here is a concrete example to clarify further: a marketplace application may request the Microsoft Graph
Sites.ReadWrite.All
delegated permission, and the user might consent to it, but the business service using the app should still be restricted to the site collections the user has access to. In this case, SharePoint Online (the resource server) validates the actual access of the user for which the token was requested and does not blindly trust the
Sites.ReadWrite.All
scope injected by Entra ID in the token. The same considerations apply to your own APIs.

Privileged Access Management
(

)and

Introducing PAM and red tenant architectures
PAM is a cybersecurity strategy and
set of technologies designed to control, monitor, and secure access to critical systems.
PAM helps minimize the risk of security breaches by enforcing the principle of least privilege, providing just-in-time and just-enough access, auditing privileged actions, and isolating credentials in secure vaults.
At this stage, the Microsoft ecosystem provides a vaultless PAM model as there is no integrated credential vault with password rotation and session isolation, which is why third parties such as CyberArk and Thycotic are often used in addition to the Microsoft stack. Talking about these is beyond the scope of this book, but be aware that they are the usual suspects in many companies.
Every serious organization has a PAM strategy that belongs to the broader
Identity and Governance Administration
(
IGA
) concept. However, the
extent to which you implement a full-fledged IGA or PAM, or something more basic, often depends on the industry and your risk appetite.
Implementing IGA is crucial for
strengthening security, supporting regulatory compliance, and boosting operational efficiency.
The core features of Entra ID’s IGA, made available by the Entra Suite plan, include user onboarding and offboarding, policy-driven permission assignments through entitlement management, and effective recertification via
access reviews. Together, these capabilities strengthen the overall PAM approach.
Now, let’s explore
Enhanced Security Admin Environment
(
ESAE
) and
Enterprise Access Model
(
EAM
), which are
PAM-friendly
architectures.
Microsoft introduced the concept of
red forest
about a decade ago, which targeted Active Directory and mostly focused on on-premises
environments. Red forest is part of the ESAE practice. The red forest concept relies on the following tiers:
Tier 0
: This tier represents the
crown jewels of your identity assets. Systems
such as
Active Directory Domain Services
(
AD DS
),
Public Key Infrastructure
(
PKI
),
AD FS
,
Privileged Access Workstation
(
PAW
), and highly privileged accounts (for example, domain admin), are
part of Tier 0. In the red forest
architecture, this tier is fully managed by a separate Active Directory. Administrators receive PAWs to perform their administrative work and use another regular device for non-administrative work, such as writing docs and collaborating with others.
Tier 1
: This tier includes member servers (application servers, SQL servers, and so on), as well as the server admin accounts. It basically includes all the servers except those belonging to tier 0.
Tier 2
: This tier includes user workstations and help desk support tools, as well as accounts needed to manage
tier 2 assets. Tier 2 accounts cannot access tier 1 or tier 0 assets.
While ESAE is now considered legacy—though still in use—it has been succeeded by EAM, which has become the standard for modern and cloud-native environments since around 2020. EAM builds on some of ESAE’s foundational principles, such as the tiered architecture, but redefines the tiers based on logical boundaries such as the control plane (former tier 0), management plane, and data/workload plane (former tier 1), as well as user access and app access (former tier 2). Despite the shift in structure, EAM shares the same objective as ESAE: to uphold the principle of least privilege and enforce strong governance over all high-privilege activities. Another key distinction between EAM and ESAE is that in EAM, high-privilege admin accounts reside strictly within the control plane, and the zero-trust security model is strictly enforced using CAPs.
While EAM is not prescriptive about using a single- or multi-tenant approach, an emerging trend consists of using a
separate
red tenant
to control and manage all control plane assets. Red tenant admin users leverage Entra ID’s cross-tenant access capabilities to gain access
to the
blue tenant
(workforce/resource tenant), where roles are effectively granted. Using separate tenants enforces stronger isolation between the control plane and the other layers, as well as dependencies. For example, the Intune admin in the workforce tenant only exists in that tenant, and as such is fully isolated from the red tenant. It also allows us to make sure PAWs can only be used by red tenant users, and the workforce tenant would block any office user using regular machines to perform administration tasks using CAPs. Admin users typically log in to PAWs using strong authentication mechanisms, such as FIDO2 keys, and receive their privileges (just in time and just enough) through a PIM session.
Figure 9.17
illustrates what EAM may look like using a red tenant:
Figure 9.17 – Example of what EAM with red tenant may look like
It is important to understand that there is no definitive rule for determining which service belongs to which layer.
Figure 9.17
illustrates a sample set of Azure services categorized under the workload/data plane; however, this is by no means exhaustive. As a general guideline, any service that hosts data or runs workloads is considered part of the workload/data plane. Services that support the operation and administration of the environment fall under the management plane. Components enabling user and application access are part of the user and app access layer. The red tenant is strictly limited to core identity services and is responsible for managing highly privileged accounts.
However, as is often the case, putting theory into practice can be challenging. In
Figure 9.17
, a clear tension emerges: Microsoft Intune is included in the red tenant to manage and enforce controls on PAWs. While Intune is theoretically part of the management plane, the red tenant is intended to be strictly limited to control plane assets. This represents a necessary deviation from the model, as PAWs cannot reasonably be managed by an Intune instance residing in a different tenant.
While the use of PAWs enhances security, it can be seen as a logistical and financial burden—particularly at scale—since it often involves provisioning and managing multiple physical devices per administrator. It also means that administrators may need to carry two separate devices when commuting, which can be inconvenient.
As a potential alternative—albeit with a lower
security posture—a
Virtual Privileged Access Workstation
(
VPAW
) restricted to red tenant users only and hosted on Azure Virtual Desktop can serve as a more flexible solution.
The company
glueckkanja
has an Azure Marketplace
product called
Managed Red Tenant
that is moving in that direction, which may be worth evaluating depending on your organization’s needs and risk tolerance.

.
Zooming in on CSPM
CSPM is a proactive approach that consists of continuously assessing the security posture of an environment. CSPM
ensures the overall configuration adheres to the best security practices. Any deviation is flagged, and recommendations are made to remediate non-compliant resources.

Working with Azure Policy in practice
We previously introduced Azure Policy in
Chapter 2
,
Solution Architecture
. As a reminder, Azure Policy is a foundational
component of compliance, continuously evaluating your environment and proactively blocking or identifying non-compliant deployments. Policies are defined with specific effects—such as
Audit
,
Deny
, or

(see
https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/governance/policy/concepts/effect-basics
for the complete list)—and are applied to a chosen scope, such as a management group or subscription. Concrete examples of policies could be the following:
Ensuring that subnets have a default network security group attached
Ensuring that Azure Storage and Azure SQL have public access denied
Ensuring that encryption in transit is enabled for services where it’s still possible to expose port
80
(HTTP)
All of these rules, with a
Deny
effect, would prevent any non-compliant workload from being deployed.

Zooming in on CWPP
CSPM and CWPP are closely related, but
the key distinction lies in their focus: CSPM takes a proactive approach by continuously evaluating security posture and configurations to prevent attacks, whereas CWPP focuses on threat detection at runtime.
In Azure, Microsoft Defender for Cloud is the cornerstone of CWPP, along with other Defender options.

All Defender variants integrate directly with Microsoft XDR or Sentinel—tools used to support SecOps activities, which we will cover later in this chapter. In addition, most Defender offerings also contribute to CSPM by providing proactive recommendations to further harden the protected services. Microsoft Defender for Identity primarily covers Active Directory and AD FS to identify and detect threats against these on-premises infrastructures.
Defender for Resource Manager focuses on interactions with the

ARM

action performed with the Azure portal, the Azure CLI, Azure PowerShell, and infrastructure as code. It can detect anomalies such as impossible travel (for example, an action from the US at 9 a.m. followed by another from France an hour later), the use of exploit toolkits, and malicious virtual machine extensions.
Defender for Databases is centered around SQL Server (all forms: Azure SQL, SQL Managed Instance, and self-hosted SQL servers), Cosmos DB, as well as other relational database engines. It protects against SQL injections, access from unusual data centers, brute-force attacks, and so on.
Defender for APIs is particularly valuable given the central role of APIs in modern architectures. It detects anomalies such as unusual spikes in API usage or irregular payload sizes by comparing them to baseline traffic patterns. It also checks whether the API endpoints are subject to authorization. Additionally, it can classify the types of data handled by your APIs, helping you understand what is exposed to consumers.
Defender for AI helps protect against prompt injections, wallet attacks, and the exposure of sensitive data to LLMs, among other things. Wallet attacks, in particular, can be mitigated using the AI Gateway pattern discussed in
Chapter 8
, Artificial Intelligence Architecture
. Enabling Defender for APIs on an API that shields the LLM using the AI Gateway pattern can be effective, not because of Defender’s features themselves, but because the LLM stays hidden while the proxy API is protected.
Both Defender for Storage and Defender for Endpoint offer malware protection, but Defender for Endpoint provides much broader coverage for virtual machines, with the ability to detect a wide range of threats occurring within those environments. Needless to say that Defender for Storage is particularly interesting when using Azure Storage’s SFTP feature. Blobs are tagged once scanned, and the scan results can be captured using Event Grid for further actions. Note that at this stage, Defender for Storage does not use sandboxing, which is a way to execute suspicious files into an isolated environment, enabling advanced threat protection.
Defender for Containers protects against reconnaissance activities, lateral movements, and crypto-mining, among other things. Additionally, Defender for Containers performs container image scanning, including daily re-scans. Microsoft has written a simulation script that generates attacks from within an AKS cluster. The script is available here:
https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/Defender-for-Cloud-Attack-Simulation/refs/heads/main/simulation.py
.
Defender for DNS should move to Defender for Servers with the same protection coverage. It protects against communication with malicious DNS resolvers and malicious domains, anomalous protocols, and network
patterns. A threat that Defender for DNS cannot detect on its own is the so-called dangling DNS, which occurs when a malicious party takes over a subdomain. In short, if you have a web app hosted at a subdomain such as
contoso.azurewebsites.net
and a corresponding CNAME record in your DNS, deleting the app without removing the CNAME leaves a vulnerability. A malicious actor could register a new App Service with the same subdomain, effectively hijacking your DNS entry, which makes you lose control over the content of the subdomain. Defender for App Service helps mitigate as it includes dangling DNS detection.
Although using Azure Firewall as a DNS proxy isn’t strictly part of workload protection, it remains a valuable complementary approach for routing DNS traffic through Azure Firewall.
Given the fast-paced evolution of the Defender ecosystem, it’s important to regularly reassess its capabilities when defining and implementing a CWPP strategy.
You can visit the following link for a comprehensive view of the security alerts generated by the various Defender options:
https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/defender-for-cloud/alerts-reference
.
It’s worth noting that investing in CSPM is far more effective than relying solely on CWPP. CSPM takes a proactive approach by continuously verifying that security best practices are enforced across your environments, helping to prevent cybersecurity threats and vulnerabilities. CWPP serves as a complementary layer, but it should not be your only line of defense.
Zooming in on DevSecOps
Because CI/CD was already introduced in
Chapter 2
,
Solution Architecture
, this section will focus exclusively on security practices.
The purpose of DevSecOps is to integrate security practices into the DevOps or GitOps process. It is based on the shift-left principle, which
consists of shifting some responsibilities to development teams and making sure to tackle security concerns as early as possible in the development life cycle.
DevOps and GitOps both heavily rely on automation. As mentioned in the introduction, security teams often operate in silos, with approval processes relying on tickets and occurring outside the DevOps pipeline. The core objective of DevSecOps is to break down these barriers by embedding security checks and approvals directly into the automation workflow.
The most effective way to achieve this is by involving security analysts directly in the CI/CD approval process. This means having them participate in peer reviews and approve or reject security-related changes through pull requests. To streamline this workflow, quality gates can be established to ensure that no solution is promoted to higher environments until it meets the required security and compliance standards.
Figure 9.23
shows different tools and DevSecOps practices that should be enforced in any organization:
Figure 9.23 – DevSecOps
The shift-left principle involves conducting early security checks in the development life cycle, primarily through
Software Composition Analysis
(
SCA
) and SAST. Both
techniques analyze source code to detect potential vulnerabilities and
provide remediation guidance. These scans are typically integrated into build pipelines to catch issues before deployment. They generate reports that typically include severity ratings or risk levels that can be used to define quality gates.
In practice, these reports are often overlooked unless strict quality gates are enforced. Apart from tools like PSRules that scan IaC artefacts, Azure does not provide native SAST or SCA frameworks. Third-party tools such as Snyk and AppScan can be integrated to fill this gap. Snyk, in particular, offers strong capabilities for scanning container images—an essential complement to source code analysis to ensure end-to-end security.
Once the code is built and meets the required quality standards, it should be deployed. Ideally, DAST tools and a strong CSPM
solution should be integrated from the development environment onward. This enables early detection of runtime vulnerabilities and misconfigurations, reinforcing security throughout the application life cycle. As we have seen in the
Zooming in on CSPM
section, only Azure Policy and the Foundational plan of Defender are already impressive from that perspective. For AKS-hosted workloads, policies are defined in Azure Policy and enforced by the Gatekeeper admission controller, ensuring deployments are compliant with security best practices.
For those willing to go the extra mile, enabling runtime protection from the development environment provides enhanced visibility into potential live threats as workloads run. In the Azure ecosystem, this can be achieved through Defender for Cloud workload protection, with Defender for Containers offering specialized coverage for containerized environments, as we have seen in our
Zooming in on CWPP
section. An alternative, though not mutually exclusive, is to rely on
Runtime Application Self-Protection
(
RASP
) to further strengthen the security level of applications through context-aware
agents, but this practice is not common and is often overkill if all the other tools mentioned in this section are already in use.
Lastly, Defender for DevOps is yet another option that lets you plug Defender into your SDLC solutions (GitHub and Azure DevOps for now). Once onboarded, Defender will scan both the infrastructure code and the entire repositories and pipelines, providing not only code recommendations but also insights for the DevOps environments themselves.

Zooming in on SecOps
At the very end of the application life
cycle comes SecOps, which is a practice aimed at detecting, responding to, and mitigating security threats. The primary purpose of SecOps is to continuously monitor the live environment. We leverage CSPM upstream as a way to proactively prevent misconfigurations and vulnerabilities, and CWPP and SecOps downstream to monitor the
live environment, as well as to generate and handle security incidents.

Extended Detection and Response
(
XDR
) is ensured by Microsoft XDR, a unified solution that correlates signals
coming from all the Defender products. Its portal is the one-stop shop for every SecOps team member, as it also integrates Azure Sentinel.
However, Sentinel is still
considered a SIEM/SOAR solution, used to connect various data sources to ingest log data and receive security incidents generated by Defender. The logical sequence to ingest and work with data in Sentinel is shown in

Once you install a provider using Content Hub, you can configure its corresponding data connector(s) to ingest the data you want to retrieve. Each provider comes with a default set of analytic rules and playbooks, which help you analyze and react to ingested data.
There are two types of rules, both written in KQL: scheduled
and
Near-Real-Time
(
NRT

Data is ingested into Log Analytics, and it is considered best practice to work with a dedicated workspace for Sentinel.
The volume of ingested data can be substantial, potentially resulting in high ingestion costs. It’s important to carefully choose only the data sources that are truly relevant to your environment. One of the key data sources commonly integrated with Microsoft Sentinel is Entra ID. It provides valuable insights to detect credential-based attacks such as password spraying and credential stuffing, as well as anomalies in credential usage. To fully benefit from this data source, you must have at least a P1 or P2 Entra ID license.
Another essential data source when using virtual machines (IaaS) is Defender for Servers, especially when combined with Defender for Endpoint and other virtual machine-specific protection mechanisms. Since the attack surface of virtual machines is typically broader than that of PaaS services, it’s crucial to enable these Defender solutions and ingest their data and alerts into Sentinel to gain a comprehensive view of your infrastructure’s security posture.
Responding to incidents involves automation rules that allow the automatic assignment of an owner, a tag, a severity, and to respond with concrete actions using playbooks.
Playbooks are based on Logic Apps workflows, which give you all the flexibility in terms of how you want to handle and respond to an incident.
The challenge with Sentinel is finding the right balance between noise—too many false positives—and silence—actual
threats not being detected and handled. Setting up Sentinel and connecting data sources can be done in minutes, but the actual work resides in the setup of analytical rules and playbooks. Additionally, SecOps team members should connect to the XDR portal on a daily basis.
Third-party solutions such as Splunk and QRadar are also commonly used in SOAR/SIEM and broader SecOps scenarios. In such cases, they are typically integrated with Azure Event Hubs to ingest logs and perform detection and response activities accordingly.

Diving deeper into WAF policies
Azure WAF policies consist of both managed
rules and custom rules. These policies can be associated with either Azure Front Door (serving as a global WAF) or Application Gateway (acting as a regional WAF). Each policy
can operate in either
prevention
mode, where malicious
requests are blocked, or
detection
mode, where traffic is monitored but not actively blocked.
A recommended best practice is to enable WAF policies in detection mode early in the development life cycle. This allows teams to review WAF logs, identify potential policy violations, and provide actionable feedback to development teams. However, in practice, WAF logs are often overlooked, which can result in unexpected disruptions when switching to prevention mode—potentially causing applications to malfunction. Continuous monitoring of WAF violations is essential, as false positives can occur and may lead to legitimate traffic being blocked unnecessarily.

(

A WAF policy can be associated with different scopes within Application Gateway—globally at the gateway level (applying to all applications), at the listener level, or per path-based rule. More specific scopes take precedence over the broader scope. This granularity offers significant flexibility to tailor

Diving deeper into Azure Firewall
Azure Firewall was first introduced in
Chapter 3
,
Infrastructure Design
, where we also deployed one as part of our use case. In this section, we will take a deeper dive into Azure Firewall and explore the key concepts and configurations that are essential to understand when working with this service.
First, it’s important to remember that
Azure Firewall is a fully cloud-native service that integrates seamlessly with the broader Azure ecosystem. It supports full management through infrastructure as code, offers zone redundancy, and can scale automatically based on CPU usage driven by network traffic.
Azure Firewall is a stateful service with a built-in
Intrusion Detection System
(
IDS
)/
Intrusion Prevention System
(
IPS
) or, in short,
IDPS
capabilities. It
supports the definition of both application rules (layer 7) and network rules (layer 4), and it allows
for the configuration of both
Destination Network Address Translation
(
DNAT
) and
Source Network Address Translation
(
SNAT
) rules.
Firewall rules are part of a
hierarchical structure. Each rule type belongs to a rule collection of a certain type. Rule collections represent a set of rules and are themselves part of rule collection groups. Each policy has three default rule collection groups, which are
DefaultDnatRuleCollectionGroup
,
DefaultNetworkRuleCollectionGroup
, and
DefaultApplicationRuleCollectionGroup
. Firewall policies can also be linked to a parent policy. All policies can be managed with Firewall Manager.
The rule-processing logic is based on priority numbers, from lowest (highest priority) to highest (lowest priority) number, but Azure Firewall always first processes DNAT rules, then network rules, and then application rules. So, you can influence the processing logic by setting your own priorities, but it will always follow the sequence: DNAT ➔ Network ➔ Application
.
Microsoft describes the full logic here:
https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/firewall/rule-processing
.
Make sure to get acquainted with this and organize your groups, collections, and rules appropriately to make sure the system remains manageable at scale. It is also important to consider the service limits in terms of how many rules you can have within a rule collection and how many groups you can have in a policy. Consult this reference for more information:
https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/azure-resource-manager/management/azure-subscription-service-limits
. You should return to this page whenever you design anything in Azure to anticipate possible limits and make sure you design future-proof architectures.

google.com

cannot inspect the full URL or the contents of the request, including headers or the body, since they are encrypted. As a result, it cannot detect a malicious header because it has no visibility into the encrypted payload.

You may have noticed the use of the
--insecure

This brings us to a very important aspect of TLS inspection. Although this allows a full inspection of the HTTP request payload, the price to pay is that all callers must trust the certificates issued by a private CA. However, this is not always possible as many Azure services do not allow you to inject a custom CA.
Figure 9.40
shows how TLS inspection changed our setup:
Figure 9.40 – How TLS inspection works
With TLS inspection enabled, the firewall is not just forwarding the request but is the actual man in the middle. There are two HTTP requests going on. The first one is between the source virtual machine and Azure Firewall, and the second one is between Azure Firewall and the actual destination. This is why the firewall is able to decrypt the payload. This means that the CA used by Azure Firewall to issue the intermediate certificates must be trusted on the calling side. For our lab, we could afford to ignore certificate errors, but this of course would not be possible in a real-world situation. Moreover, this CA is typically not a public well-known CA, so it is not trusted by default.
A few Azure services allow you to inject custom CAs, such as App Service, hosted in an
App Service Environment
(
ASE
), AKS, and basically
any container-based services where you can mount the CA as a volume. Virtual machines also support the injection of custom CAs.
Beyond trusting the CA, TLS inspection also has an impact on performance because of the decryption, inspection, and
reencryption of the payload. When using a custom firewall, you must ensure it scales according to the volume of traffic, else it could quickly become a bottleneck, causing significant latencies.
I strongly advise only enabling TLS inspection for FQDNs that you do not trust or that could lead to a possible data leakage. I strongly discourage enabling TLS inspection for everything, especially when targeting services such as Entra ID.
In any case, you should use TLS inspection with care and evaluate the impacts first. To learn more about Azure Firewall certificates for TLS inspection, you can refer to this online documentation:
https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/firewall/premium-certificates
.
CASB solutions in a nutshell
CASB solutions are closely linked to the
zero-trust security model, which aims at verifying every activity happening inside and outside the corporate network, since nothing should be trusted by default. CASB contributes to zero trust in that it acts as a bridge between users and cloud services, monitoring which services are accessed, and protecting against threats and data leakage. It is mostly used on user devices where a CASB client such as Netskope intercepts all traffic destined for the internet (SaaS solutions, etc.) and performs TLS inspection to prevent possible data leakage and allow/deny the user device to reach out to the destination.
Microsoft ships with Defender for Cloud Apps, but Netskope and Zscaler are commonly used as well. Although CASB is initially centered around user traffic, it starts to be more commonly used for server-side components accessing cloud services.
Zscaler, for example, provides
the
Zscaler Internet Access
(
ZIA

Summary
This chapter presented a comprehensive view of security architecture in Azure, emphasizing its critical role across identity, data, infrastructure, workloads, and operations. It highlighted the need to modernize security practices through automation, risk-based governance, and closer alignment between development, operations, and security teams.
Identity was addressed as the new perimeter, with Entra ID providing strong access controls via Conditional Access, PIM, and support for modern protocols such as OAuth 2.0 and OIDC. Data security was examined through encryption at rest, in transit, and in use, along with key management strategies such as BYOK, HYOK, and managed HSM. Governance features such as Microsoft Purview, audit logs, versioning, and dynamic masking were also discussed to ensure data integrity. The chapter contrasted CSPM and CWPP, showing how CSPM enables proactive configuration compliance while CWPP focuses on runtime protection. DevSecOps was reinforced as a key practice, with integration of SAST, DAST, and container scanning into CI/CD pipelines. SecOps was covered through Microsoft Sentinel and Defender XDR, using playbooks, analytic rules, and automation to detect and respond to incidents. The chapter also explored Azure-native appliances such as Azure Firewall and WAF, including TLS inspection and its operational implications. Finally, we highlighted the growing use of CASB solutions in the context of zero trust. Overall, the chapter emphasized that effective cloud security depends on adopting native capabilities, embracing automation, and aligning security models with the cloud-native paradigm.

Closing Thoughts and Next Steps
Writing a book is about making choices
. This is especially true when the ambition is to depict the vast and ever-evolving Azure landscape. Some topics warrant deep technical dives, while others can only be briefly touched upon, not due to a lack of importance but simply because of scope and space constraints. Throughout this book, we’ve made deliberate choices to balance depth with breadth, to provide hands-on insights where possible, and to offer architectural thinking that resonates with real-world Azure practices. The result is a guided journey through Azure that equips architects, engineers, and decision-makers with solid knowledge and actionable insights—without claiming to be exhaustive.
Across the chapters, the book established a logical progression, starting with the
fundamentals of cloud architecture
and the diversity of architect roles (
Chapter 1
). The concept of Azure maps was introduced early to structure decision-making, enabling readers to navigate Azure services based on technical intent.
Chapter 2
built on this by defining the Solution Architecture Map and categorizing workloads (for example, SoRs and SoIs), while also introducing maturity models and governance strategies to manage evolving concerns pragmatically.
The narrative then moved on to
core infrastructure and platform services
, starting with foundational practices in networking, monitoring, HA/DR, and security-first designs (
Chapter 3
).
Chapters 4
and
5
zoomed into the
container landscape
, offering a comprehensive view of AKS—including its network and security intricacies—and evaluating simpler container alternatives such as Azure Container Apps and ACI. These chapters helped readers weigh complexity against agility, and showed how technologies such as Istio and Calico can support multi-tenant SaaS platforms within AKS.
Shifting toward
application design
,
Chapter 6
addressed the cloud development experience, emphasizing the importance of the Azure ecosystem when developing solutions. It gave practical guidance on local development and the must-have skills each developer and application architect should have.
Chapter 7
broadened the lens to
data architectures
, dissecting ingestion, storage, and real-time analytics pipelines. From OLTP stores such as Cosmos DB to OLAP services such as Synapse and Azure Data Explorer, it painted a layered view of modern data ecosystems. Infrastructure enablers such as virtual network integration, managed virtual networks, and SHIR were also covered, including hybrid scenarios, grounding the discussion in enterprise reality.
Chapter 8
explored the rise of
AI in Azure
, contrasting the use of traditional and generative AI. It introduced vector databases, retrieval-augmented generation, orchestration frameworks, and the concept of agentic AI, while emphasizing architectural choices around cost, precision, and scalability. We purposely focused on architectural patterns only and avoided any hands-on practical examples, given the fast-moving pace of the AI realm that would have rendered code samples obsolete by the time the book reached publication. The aim was to give readers a durable foundation for AI solution design, rather than transient implementation guidance.
Finally,
Chapter 9
delivered a
holistic view of security architecture
, touching on identity, data, workloads, and operations. It reinforced the importance of DevSecOps and SecOps practices, explored the interplay between CSPM and CWPP, and emphasized zero-trust principles through native Azure capabilities such as Entra ID, Microsoft Purview, Sentinel, and Defender XDR. Automation and alignment across teams emerged as essential themes, particularly as organizations mature their cloud security postures.
Altogether, this book is a compass more than an encyclopedia. It provides the maps, the questions, and the trade-offs—not always the definitive answers. Readers are invited to adapt, extend, and question its content as they chart their own journey through the Azure cloud.
