The tipping point
The journey from DevOps maturity to platform engineering is an evolutionary process, where an organization gradually expands its capabilities and adapts its structures to meet new demands and scale efficiently. This journey is typically not a linear progression but is punctuated by a key moment we will call the
tipping point
. This is the moment when the need for a transition to platform engineering becomes inevitable and urgent. This section delves into what constitutes a tipping point in the context of platform engineering, the signs indicating its arrival, and the benefits of recognizing it early.
Outgrowing traditional embedded DevOps models
In the context of platform engineering, the tipping point can be defined as a critical juncture where the existing DevOps practices no longer sufficiently support the scalability, efficiency, and agility needs of an organization. It signifies a moment of strategic realignment, where the benefits of shifting to a platform-oriented approach significantly outweigh the cost of maintaining the status quo.This tipping point can be triggered by various factors, including escalating operational complexities, increasing demands for speed and agility, the need for better standardization, and the quest for superior developer experience. Recognizing this tipping point is vital as it informs the organization's decision to pivot toward a more sustainable, scalable, and efficient model of software delivery and operation – platform engineering.To better understand this timing, we only need to imagine a scaling organization that consistently onboards new engineering members and deploys new services. As shown in the following figure, when we deploy a new service, there are usually many aspects involved besides coding, such as automations, infrastructure provisioning, and more. On the other hand, in an ideal platform world, the only thing a development team would have to do besides coding would be to integrate with the platform APIs, in order to deploy their new service.

This additional cost can come in many shapes and forms. There are several indicators that an organization has reached the tipping point toward platform engineering:
Rising operational complexities
: As an organization grows, so do its operational intricacies. If these complexities start overwhelming the existing DevOps practices and teams, it's a clear sign that a shift toward platform engineering is due.
Increasing deployment bottlenecks
: If software deployment becomes a constant bottleneck, hampering delivery speed and productivity, it's an indication that a more efficient platform approach is needed.
Scaling challenges
: The inability of current DevOps practices to effectively scale in line with the organization's growth often serves as a tipping point. This can be seen in increasing system latencies, rising infrastructure costs, or difficulties in managing larger teams and workflows.
Standardization needs
: If there's a growing demand for better standardization across teams and processes to improve consistency, repeatability, and governance, it's a clear sign that a shift to platform engineering is beneficial.
Demand for superior developer experience
: A rising need for improved developer experience, such as better tooling, smoother workflows, and enhanced productivity, also signals a tipping point.
Need for specialized skills
: As companies grow, their tooling complexity also grows and requires a set of advanced skills. This comes with a higher cost of recruiting and retaining specialized talent, as well as an increased effort needed to continuously stay at the edge of technological advancements.

Recognizing the tipping point early offers several benefits:
Firstly, it allows for proactive planning and a smoother transition to platform engineering, reducing potential disruptions. It facilitates a more organized evolution of DevOps practices into a platform-oriented model, enabling a gradual and structured change rather than a rushed reaction to escalating issues.
Secondly, early recognition helps align stakeholders toward the new strategic direction. This alignment is vital for securing the necessary support and resources for the transition.
Finally, it prevents the unnecessary accumulation of technical debt. If the tipping point is not recognized in time, organizations may continue to build upon an increasingly shaky foundation, leading to unsustainable technical debt. Early recognition allows organizations to switch courses before this debt escalates too far. In this way, it promotes more sustainable and scalable growth.
Signs that your organization is ready for a platform team
Increasing complexity in DevOps processes is a major indicator that your organization is ready for a transition to a platform team. The complexity here refers not just to technical intricacy but also to organizational and process-related complications.From a technical standpoint, as your product or service offerings grow, so do the dependencies and interactions among various software components. This can lead to escalating complexities in managing and operating these components cohesively. For instance, as microservices architecture becomes more prevalent, managing individual services, their interactions, and their dependencies can quickly grow complex.On the process front, as your development and operations teams expand, coordinating their efforts becomes increasingly challenging. The sheer number of tasks, ranging from code integration and testing to deployment and monitoring, can become overwhelming.From an organizational perspective, as more teams and roles are introduced into the DevOps life cycle, the coordination overhead escalates. The necessity of communication and collaboration across diverse roles – from developers, testers, and operations staff to security and compliance teams – adds another layer of complexity.Recognizing these signs of escalating complexity and responding with the introduction of a platform team can bring significant benefits. The platform team can help streamline these complexities by providing a unified platform that simplifies workflows, enhances tooling, and standardizes processes, thus improving efficiency and productivity across the board.A growing need for standardization and efficiency in DevOps processes is a sign that your organization is ripe for a platform team.Standardization brings consistency and predictability to the development life cycle, reducing errors and inefficiencies. It involves defining common practices, guidelines, and tools for tasks such as coding, testing, deployment, and monitoring. However, as the scale and complexity of operations grow, maintaining this standardization can become challenging.Similarly, efficiency in DevOps is crucial for accelerating software delivery and improving resource utilization. However, without a dedicated focus on improving workflows, tooling, and processes, efficiency gains can be hard to achieve and sustain.A platform team can directly address these needs. It can work toward standardizing tools, practices, and processes across the organization, thereby reducing variability and improving predictability. Moreover, by focusing on optimizing workflows and automating routine tasks, it can significantly enhance the overall efficiency of the DevOps life cycle.
Steps to take after identifying the tipping point
Once you've recognized the tipping point, you can follow these steps:
Build a strong business case for a platform team
: This involves articulating the potential benefits, costs, and risks associated with the transition:
Start by emphasizing the value proposition of a platform team. Highlight how it can help manage increasing complexity, alleviate bottlenecks, and promote standardization and efficiency in your DevOps processes. Use quantitative metrics wherever possible. For example, illustrate how a platform team can speed up software delivery times, reduce operational errors, or improve resource utilization.
Next, outline the expected costs of setting up a platform team. This includes not just the direct costs, such as hiring or training personnel, but also indirect costs, such as the effort required to redefine processes and workflows or to integrate existing tools and systems into the new platform.
Also, acknowledge the potential risks and challenges associated with the transition. These could range from resistance to change among existing teams to potential disruptions in workflows during the transition phase.
Finally, provide a roadmap outlining how the platform team would be set up and integrated into the organization's existing structure. This should include tentative timelines, key milestones, and success criteria, thereby providing a clear and realistic path forward.
Identify initial platform engineering projects
: This is the next step after establishing the case for a platform team. These projects should be chosen strategically to demonstrate the value of the platform team quickly and tangibly:
Start by identifying areas where the platform team can make a significant impact. These could be areas where bottlenecks are most prevalent, or where standardization and efficiency gains could bring substantial benefits. Examples could include automating deployment processes, standardizing testing procedures, or setting up a self-service portal for environment provisioning.
Consider the complexity and duration of potential projects. Initial projects should ideally be manageable in scope and achievable within a short timeframe to quickly demonstrate value. However, they should also be significant enough to bring noticeable improvements to DevOps processes.
Align the initial projects with the organization's broader strategic goals
: Whether it's accelerating time-to-market, improving software quality, or enhancing customer experience, make sure that the projects contribute to these overarching objectives.
Set up the platform engineering team structure
: This involves defining roles, responsibilities, and workflows for the new team:
Firstly, identify the key roles required in the platform team. These could include platform engineers who will build and maintain the platform, product managers who will define the platform's vision and roadmap, and possibly DevOps engineers who will help integrate the platform into existing DevOps processes.
Define clear responsibilities for each role. This helps ensure that everyone on the team understands their duties and the expectations placed upon them. It also helps to prevent overlaps or gaps in responsibilities, which could lead to confusion or inefficiencies down the line.
Outline the workflows and processes the platform team will follow
: This should include how they will collaborate with other teams, how they will handle requests and feedback, and how they will track and report on their progress. This structure helps ensure that the platform team operates smoothly and integrates effectively into the larger organization.

A lean approach to visualizing value – VSM
In the journey toward platform engineering, one must clearly understand and articulate the value that the platform will provide. This can be achieved through a lean approach known as
Value Stream Mapping
(
VSM
). This tool offers a visual representation of a process or system from start to finish, highlighting the value added at each step and identifying potential areas of waste or inefficiency. In the context of transitioning from DevOps to platform engineering, VSM can play a pivotal role in identifying high-value opportunities for the platform team.
Introduction to VSM
VSM is a lean management technique used for analyzing and designing the flow of materials and information required to bring a product or service from its inception to the customer. It graphically illustrates, step-by-step, the activities involved in delivering the product or service, allowing organizations to see where delays are occurring, where value is added, and where waste can be identified and eliminated.In software development, VSM can be used to visualize the whole life cycle from the feature request from the user until a new feature is finally deployed in production and the value is realized by the customer. In a company's DevOps journey, this can be used to identify development bottlenecks and inefficiencies, as shown in the following figure.

In the context of platform engineering, the
product
is the platform itself, and the
customer
is usually the internal development teams that use the platform to build and deploy applications.The principles of VSM in platform engineering are essentially the same as in other contexts: it's about visualizing the entire life cycle of platform service delivery, identifying value-adding activities, and finding ways to minimize waste and inefficiency.VSM can offer the following significant benefits when transitioning to platform engineering:
It provides a clear visual representation of current DevOps processes. This helps identify areas of inefficiency, redundancies, or bottlenecks that could be addressed through platform engineering solutions.
VSM helps in defining the scope of platform engineering projects. Highlighting the value-adding activities and waste in the current processes can guide the platform team in deciding which services or capabilities to focus on.
VSM promotes a customer-centric approach. It helps the platform team understand the needs of their internal
customers
(that is, the development teams) better, fostering a culture of continuous improvement and user-centric design in the platform.
VSM aids in communication and collaboration. It provides a common language and framework for discussing processes and workflows, facilitating better collaboration between the platform team and other stakeholders.
To illustrate the power of VSM, let's consider an organization transitioning from DevOps to platform engineering. The organization decided to apply VSM to its software release process, which was causing delays and hindering the pace of innovation.They started by mapping out the entire release process from code check-in to production deployment. The map highlighted several areas of inefficiency, including manual testing processes, approval delays, and resource provisioning bottlenecks.These insights guided the formation of their platform engineering strategy. They decided to focus their initial platform projects on automating testing processes, streamlining approval workflows, and setting up a self-service environment provisioning system. These initiatives were able to significantly reduce the lead time for software releases, thereby demonstrating the value of their platform team and setting a solid foundation for future platform engineering initiatives.
Implementing VSM
Creating a value stream map involves several steps:
Identifying the product or service
: In the context of platform engineering, this would typically be a specific service or capability provided by the platform.
Defining the start and end points
: This constitutes the boundaries of the process being mapped. It could be from the point of a developer's request to the delivery of a platform service.
Gathering information
: This involves collecting detailed data about every step in the process, including the time taken, resources used, people involved, and so on.
Drawing the current state map
: This is a visual representation of the process as it currently exists, including all the steps, flows, and delays.
Analyzing the current state map
: This involves looking for waste, bottlenecks, or other opportunities for improvement.
Developing a future state map
: This is a vision of what the process could look like with the identified improvements implemented.
Creating an action plan
: This includes specific steps, responsibilities, and timelines to achieve the future state.
In the value stream map,
waste
is any step that does not add value to the end product or service from the customer's perspective. In platform engineering, these might be manual tasks, delays in approval workflows, or complexities in deployment processes.Once waste is identified, strategies should be devised to eliminate or reduce it. This could involve automating tasks, streamlining processes, or implementing better coordination mechanisms.VSM can be applied to improve each of the four pillars of platform engineering:
Developer experience
: VSM can identify areas in the developer workflow that cause friction or inefficiency. These insights can guide efforts to improve tooling, automation, or documentation, enhancing the overall developer experience.
Scalability and performance
: VSM can highlight bottlenecks or constraints in the platform that hinder scaling or performance. This can inform initiatives to optimize resource usage, improve load balancing, or design more scalable architecture.
Security and compliance
: VSM can uncover security or compliance risks in the platform delivery process. This can drive efforts to strengthen security controls, automate compliance checks, or enhance security monitoring.
Promoting best practices and standards
: VSM can reveal inconsistencies or deviations in adhering to best practices or standards. This can spur actions to standardize processes, reinforce best practices, or implement policy-as-code mechanisms.
By aligning improvements identified through VSM with these four pillars, organizations can ensure a holistic and balanced approach to their platform engineering initiatives.

From tools to platform – Building on previous success
Transitioning from DevOps to platform engineering doesn't mean starting from scratch. It's a journey that leverages the work already done, the skills already learned, and the tools already in use. The goal is to evolve these assets from serving specific projects or teams to providing a platform that benefits the entire organization. This section explores how to conduct a thorough inventory of existing DevOps practices and tools, evaluate them for their suitability for the platform, and manage the transition process efficiently.
Leveraging existing DevOps practices and tools
The first step in leveraging existing DevOps practices and tools for the platform is conducting a comprehensive inventory. An
internal service catalog
can serve as a structured repository for this inventory. It can catalog everything from infrastructure provisioning scripts to CI/CD pipelines, configuration management tools, testing frameworks, and monitoring solutions. This service catalog not only provides visibility into the tools and practices currently in use but also details their use cases, owners, users, and known issues. It's essential to involve all relevant stakeholders (such as development teams, operations, and security) in this inventory process to ensure a holistic view of the existing DevOps environment.After compiling a thorough inventory, the next step is to evaluate these tools and practices for their suitability for the platform. Criteria for evaluation might include scalability, maintainability, integration capabilities, and the tool or practice's alignment with the platform's goals and the organization's overall strategy. For example, a CI/CD tool that works well for a single team but struggles to scale across multiple teams might not be suitable for the platform. Other considerations could include the tool's support for infrastructure as code, its ability to provide self-service capabilities, and its compatibility with the organization's technology stack.Transitioning selected DevOps tools and practices to the platform involves careful planning and execution. It's crucial to ensure a smooth transition with minimal disruption to current workflows. This might involve setting up dedicated migration teams, conducting pilot migrations, and providing comprehensive training and support for users. An iterative approach is often beneficial, starting with less critical applications and gradually increasing the complexity of the applications migrated. Regular feedback loops with users can help identify any issues early and make necessary adjustments. The transition process is also an opportunity to standardize practices, optimize processes, and improve documentation, leading to a more robust and efficient platform.
Strategies for a successful transition to platform engineering
Planning the transition from DevOps to platform engineering is a strategic effort that involves significant forethought and careful decision-making. A well-crafted plan addresses questions such as which practices and tools will transition first, how the organizational structure will change, and how current workflows will be affected. It's essential to align the transition plan with business goals and ensure it gets buy-in from all relevant stakeholders. This might involve presentations, workshops, or other forms of communication that highlight the benefits of the transition. Furthermore, any potential risks associated with the transition should be identified, and appropriate mitigation strategies should be put in place. It's crucial to remember that the plan should be flexible enough to adjust to unexpected changes or challenges along the way.Executing the transition is the point where planning meets action. To ensure a smooth execution, a phased approach is often the most effective. Begin with low-risk areas, and gradually move on to more complex tools and practices. This approach enables the organization to learn from each phase, making necessary adjustments before the next. Communication during this phase is crucial to ensure that all stakeholders are aware of the progress and have an avenue to raise any concerns. It's also vital to provide the necessary training and resources to help teams adjust to the new platform-centric approach. Finally, it's crucial to celebrate wins along the way, even small ones, to maintain momentum and keep morale high.Once the transition is in progress, it's important to regularly review and improve the process. This involves analyzing the successes and challenges of each phase of the transition and determining what can be learned from them. For instance, if a specific tool's transition resulted in significant workflow disruption, it's essential to understand why this happened and how to avoid it in the future. Regular reviews also allow for ongoing risk management, enabling the organization to proactively address potential issues before they become problematic. Improving the transition process is a continuous endeavor, which underscores the need for a culture of openness, feedback, and constant learning. It's this culture that ultimately paves the way for a successful transition to platform engineering.
Sustaining success in platform engineering
Continuous improvement is the lifeblood of any successful platform engineering endeavor. In the ever-evolving landscape of software development, static practices can quickly become obsolete. Hence, it's vital to adopt a mindset of continuous improvement and iteration. This involves regular reviews of the platform's performance, efficacy, and impact on the organization's broader goals.In addition, it's essential to keep an eye on industry trends and developments. This helps to identify new tools, practices, and ideas that could further enhance your platform engineering capabilities. Furthermore, the platform engineering team should foster a culture of openness and feedback where all members can contribute ideas for improvement. This might involve iterative development practices, regular team retrospectives, or other feedback mechanisms.The goal of continuous improvement is not just about making the platform better. It's about creating a culture where the team is always striving for excellence, where learning and growth are celebrated, and where innovation is not just encouraged but expected.Just as in any other business endeavor, measuring success is crucial in platform engineering. This requires defining clear metrics that align with your organization's goals and objectives. For instance, if one of the goals of your platform engineering initiative is to reduce the time developers spend on routine tasks, a relevant metric might be the average time saved per developer per week.These metrics should be tracked regularly, with results shared with all relevant stakeholders. Regular measurement not only allows you to assess the impact of your platform engineering efforts but also helps identify areas where further improvements can be made.However, while quantitative metrics are important, don't overlook qualitative ones. For instance, developer satisfaction can be a powerful indicator of platform success. Regular surveys or informal feedback sessions can provide valuable insights into how developers perceive the platform and its impact on their work.Ultimately, tracking and measuring success is about ensuring that your platform engineering efforts are delivering real, tangible benefits to your organization. It's about proving that platform engineering isn't just a buzzword but a powerful approach that can drive efficiency, innovation, and growth. Change management – How to decommission tools and services
Change is the only constant in the tech world, and platform engineering is no exception. As your platform evolves and matures, there will be a need to decommission certain tools and services. The process of decommissioning is a significant change that can disrupt operations if not managed properly. This section discusses the importance of effective
change management
in platform engineering, with a focus on decommissioning tools and services.
The importance of effective change management
Change management is the structured approach for transitioning individuals, teams, and organizations from a current state to a desired future state. It is a critical component of any platform engineering transformation, particularly when decommissioning tools and services.According to John Kotter's
change management theory
, successful change requires eight steps, including "
establishing a sense of urgency, creating a guiding coalition, developing a vision and strategy, communicating the change vision, empowering broad-based action, generating short-term wins, consolidating gains and producing more change, and anchoring new approaches in the culture"
(Kotter, 1996).In the context of platform engineering,
decommissioning
a tool or service involves more than just shutting it down. It affects users, workflows, and processes that depend on these tools and services. Effective change management ensures that these changes are communicated and managed properly, thereby minimizing disruption and resistance while maximizing acceptance and engagement.Decommissioning tools and services come with their unique challenges. One of the most common is resistance to change. Tools and services become ingrained in people's workflows and routines. Changing them can be unsettling, and there is often resistance, even when the change is objectively beneficial.Another challenge is managing dependencies. Some tools and services have dependencies on others. Decommissioning a tool or service without understanding and addressing these dependencies can lead to unexpected problems.Communication is another significant challenge. It is crucial to communicate changes effectively to all affected parties, explaining why the change is necessary, what the impact will be, and how the change will be managed. Failure to communicate effectively can lead to confusion, resistance, and disruption.Poor change management can have several negative impacts. It can lead to confusion, resistance, and lower productivity. It can disrupt workflows and lead to errors and delays. In extreme cases, it can even result in the failure of the decommissioning project.On an organizational level, poor change management can lead to a decrease in morale and engagement. It can create a culture of fear and resistance to change, which can hinder future innovation and growth.On the other hand, effective change management can help ensure a smooth transition, with minimal disruption and maximum acceptance. It can help create a culture of adaptability and resilience, where change is not feared but embraced as an opportunity for improvement and innovation.
Strategies for effective change management
Planning for change is the first step in effective change management. This involves understanding the scope of the change, identifying the affected parties, and outlining the steps necessary for a successful transition:
To start, a comprehensive inventory of all tools and services, as well as their dependencies, should be created. This allows for a complete view of the ecosystem and aids in identifying the potential impact of decommissioning a tool or service.
Next, identify all the stakeholders, both internal and external, who will be affected by the decommissioning. This might include development teams, operations teams, product management, customers, and more.
Once these factors have been identified, a detailed plan can be crafted. This plan should outline the steps required for the decommissioning process, including timelines, responsibilities, contingency plans, and KPIs to measure the success of the change.
Effective communication is crucial in managing change. Clear, consistent, and continuous communication helps to ensure that everyone understands what is happening, why it's happening, and how it will affect them.Communication should begin as soon as the decision to decommission a tool or service has been made. The reasons for the change, the benefits it will bring, and how it will be managed should be clearly explained. Regular updates should be provided throughout the process to keep everyone informed of progress and any changes to the plan. Even in cases of little or no progress, it's better to proactively communicate and keep awareness going.A variety of communication methods should be used to ensure that the message reaches everyone. This might include email updates, team meetings, town hall meetings, intranet posts, and more.Resistance to change is natural and should be expected. Managing this resistance is key to ensuring a smooth transition.Firstly, it's important to understand the source of the resistance. Is it due to fear of the unknown? A lack of understanding about the change? Or perhaps it's due to perceived negative impacts on individuals' roles and responsibilities? Another very common source of resistance is the "not invented here" syndrome, where engineers are reluctant to use tooling they haven't built and don't know their internals.Once the source of resistance is understood, strategies can be put in place to address it. This might include additional communication, training, support, and involvement in the change process.To ensure adoption, it can be helpful to identify and work with champions of the change within the organization. These individuals can help to promote the benefits of the change, assist with training and support, and provide feedback on how the change is being received.Finally, patience and persistence are key. Change takes time, and there may be setbacks along the way. However, with careful planning, effective communication, and proactive management of resistance, the transition can be successful.
A guide to decommissioning tools and services
The transition from DevOps to platform engineering often involves decommissioning some existing tools and services. Kurt Lewin describes the change process in three steps, where you start with the status quo, a piece of ice that is hard to change. Initially, you must
unfreeze
, by creating the right environment for the change to happen. The second step is implementing the change. The third and final step is
refreezing
, where a system and processes should be put in place to make the change stick in the long term.
Figure 4.5 – The Lewin three-step change model: Unfreeze-Change-Refreeze
Identifying which ones to decommission requires careful evaluation. Here are some steps to follow to
unfreeze
:
Creating an inventory
: Start by creating a service catalog of all the existing tools and services used in different teams for their DevOps practices. This includes everything from development tools and CI/CD pipelines to monitoring systems and communication platforms.
Analyzing usage and value
: Understand for each team what DevOps issues they're trying to solve and processes to optimize. For each tool and service, analyze its usability across different teams and projects. Evaluate the value it delivers in terms of improving efficiency, reducing errors, facilitating collaboration, and so on. Tools and services that are infrequently used or do not provide significant value are potential candidates for decommissioning.
Assessing compatibility
: Evaluate the compatibility of each tool and service with the principles of platform engineering. Does it support self-service, automation, and scalability? If not, it might need some extra work to become generic rather than being replaced.
Considering dependencies
: Understand the dependencies that each tool and service has on others. Decommissioning a tool or service might have a domino effect, impacting other tools and services that depend on it.
Getting feedback
: Seek feedback from the teams that use the tools and services. Their insights can be invaluable in identifying the strengths and weaknesses of each tool and service and understanding the impact of decommissioning them.
Making the change
Once the tools and services to be used or decommissioned have been identified, it's time to start the transition or adoption process. Here's a step-by-step guide:
Notifying stakeholders
: Communicate the decision to decommission the tool or service to all stakeholders, providing the rationale, the expected benefits, and the timeline for decommissioning.
Planning the decommissioning
: Develop a detailed decommissioning plan. This should include tasks, timelines, responsibilities, and contingencies. Make sure you have a rollback plan in case of unexpected issues.
Migrating to alternatives
: If the tool or service is being replaced, migrate the users, data, and functionality to the new tool or service. Provide support and training to help users adapt to the new tool or service.
Testing the changes
: Before fully decommissioning the tool or service, test the changes to ensure everything works as expected.
Decommissioning the tool or service
: Once everything is ready, decommission the tool or service. This may involve shutting down servers, deleting data, or terminating subscriptions.
Refreeze
After the tool or service has been decommissioned, there are some post-decommissioning steps to ensure the transition is smooth:
Monitoring the impact
: Keep a close eye on the impact of the decommissioning. This includes monitoring performance, tracking issues, and observing user adoption and satisfaction.
Gathering feedback
: Solicit feedback from users on their experience with the decommissioning process and the new tool or service (if any). Use this feedback to make improvements.
Documenting the process

What becomes evident is that an IDP is not just a toolset; it's a strategic enabler that fundamentally transforms how software development is approached and executed.In recent years, Kubernetes has emerged as a pivotal component in the world of software development, earning its moniker as the

Unified orchestration
: Kubernetes offers a standardized way of orchestrating containers, regardless of where they're deployed, be it on-premises, in a public cloud, or in a hybrid environment. This universality ensures consistency and portability across diverse infrastructures.
Scalability and reliability

Extensible and modular

Ecosystem and community

Resource management
: Kubernetes-based runtime environments can automatically allocate resources such as CPU, memory, and storage to applications based on predefined policies or real-time demands. This dynamic allocation ensures optimal resource utilization and efficient application performance.
Self-healing
: One of the standout features of a Kubernetes-based runtime is its self-healing capability. If an application crashes, Kubernetes can automatically restart the failed containers, ensuring uninterrupted service.
Service discovery and load balancing
: In a microservices architecture, different service components of an application need to communicate seamlessly. MELT – The pillars of observability
Observability is typically described in terms of four main pillars, encapsulated by the acronym MELT:
Metrics
: These are numerical values that represent the current state of a system. Examples include the number of requests per second a service is handling or the memory usage of a particular component.
Events
: Events are discrete occurrences in a system that can be significant for various reasons. For instance, a sudden surge in traffic or a service going down are examples of events. A new service being deployed or upgraded is an event. Integrating those in our observability pipeline gives us contextual intelligence.
Logs
: Logs are detailed textual records that are produced by software components. They often contain information about the operations a system is performing, errors it's encountering, or any other pertinent information.
Traces
: Traces provide a pathway through the system. They show the journey of a request as it traverses various services and components, highlighting where delays or failures might occur.

In a world accustomed to plug-and-play solutions, where

Diversity in organizational needs and processes
: Every organization is unique, not just in terms of its business goals but also in terms of its development methodologies, processes, and workflows. Varying tech stacks and legacy systems
: Companies have diverse technological backgrounds. Some might be using cutting-edge tech stacks, while others might be deeply entrenched in legacy systems. An off-the-shelf IDP would struggle to cater uniformly to both these extremes and everything in between.
The importance of flexibility and customization
: An IDP is not just about tools; it's about providing an environment that boosts developer productivity and efficiency. This demands a high level of flexibility and customization, which, by definition, is challenging to provide in a standardized product. An IDP tailored to an organization can better align with the developers' workflows and needs, something an off-the-shelf solution might fall short of.
The evolving nature of development practices
: The realm of software development is constantly evolving, with new methodologies, tools, and best practices emerging rapidly. This dynamic nature makes it hard for a standardized IDP solution to stay relevant and updated without frequent and sometimes drastic overhauls.
Security and compliance concerns
: A significant facet of IDPs is ensuring security and compliance. Different organizations operate under various regulatory environments and have unique security requirements. It's almost impossible for an off-the-shelf solution to cater to this vast spectrum of security and compliance needs without extensive customization.

How opinionated should the platform be?
The design and implementation of an IDP are often influenced by a fundamental question: "
How opinionated should the platform be?
" The degree to which an IDP dictates certain workflows, technologies, or practices can significantly impact developer experience, the adaptability of the platform, and the overall efficacy of the system. Striking a balance between a highly opinionated platform (essentially a product) and a flexible system that allows for ad hoc solutions is crucial. Understanding this balance can come from understanding loosely defined flexible paths to rigidly defined ones:
Defined pathways
: A highly opinionated IDP offers clear, defined pathways for developers to follow. Such an approach brings uniformity, predictability, and, often, efficiency. Developers spend less time making decisions about tools or processes and more time focusing on coding. This can expedite onboarding, simplify training, and ensure that best practices are applied consistently.
However, the rigidity of defined pathways might stifle innovation. Developers may find it challenging to introduce new tools or practices, even if they're more suitable for specific tasks. Moreover, a one-size-fits-all approach may not cater to the nuanced needs of diverse projects.
Flexible pathways
: On the other hand, a flexible IDP offers teams the liberty to devise their workflows and integrate tools of their choice. Such flexibility can foster innovation and allow teams to adopt emerging technologies rapidly and tailor workflows to project-specific requirements.
However, excessive flexibility can lead to a fragmented tech ecosystem within an organization. It can introduce inconsistencies in how different teams operate, making collaboration and resource sharing more complicated. Additionally, managing and supporting a wide array of tools can strain the operations team and dilute the very purpose of an IDP.
Striking a balance
: The key is to find a middle ground – perhaps a semi-opinionated platform that offers defaults but allows overrides. This approach provides developers with guidance but doesn't box them into rigid pathways.
Before deciding how opinionated an IDP should be, it's essential to evaluate the current technological landscape of the company. The following are some guiding questions:
Diversity of projects
: Are the projects within the company diverse in nature, requiring different tools and workflows? Or is there a predominant type of project that can benefit from standardized processes?
Existing tech stack
: What technologies and tools are currently in use? Are they modern and efficient, or are there legacy systems that the IDP needs to accommodate or replace?
Developer preferences
: What are the preferences and expertise of the development teams? Are they more comfortable with a certain tech stack? Can the IDP offer them the best of both worlds?
Operational challenges
: Are there existing operational bottlenecks or challenges that a more opinionated (or flexible) IDP can address?
Future goals
: Where does the company see itself in the coming years in terms of tech adoption? Is there an inclination toward certain technologies or practices? What's the future roadmap of those tools like and how well does it align with the company's direction?
Striking a balance between flexibility and control
In the quest for the optimal IDP, organizations often grapple with the dilemma of flexibility versus control. While it's tempting to cater to every possible need, it's equally vital to ensure that the platform remains manageable, secure, and consistent. Understanding where to draw the line is critical to achieving an IDP that offers the right mix of adaptability and governance.Every organization is a mosaic of diverse teams and departments, each with its unique needs, tools, and workflows. Ideally, an IDP should accommodate these variations without becoming overly convoluted. To do so, continuous feedback from the internal users is key:
Survey and understand
: Regularly surveying development teams to understand their requirements, pain points, and preferences can provide valuable insights. These insights can guide the platform's evolution.
Cross-departmental collaboration
: Encourage dialog between departments. For instance, what the development team views as flexibility, the security team might see as a potential risk. Strive for solutions that address concerns from multiple vantage points.
Modular approach
: Consider designing the platform in a modular fashion. This way, different teams can plug in the tools and features they need, without cluttering the environment for others.
Flexibility is a double-edged sword. While it caters to diverse needs, excessive flexibility can introduce complications:
Tech fragmentation
: When teams can choose any tool they want, it can lead to a plethora of technologies being in use, making integrations and collaborations cumbersome.
Increased operational overheads
: Supporting a wide array of tools can strain operational teams, leading to longer response times and potential downtimes.
Inconsistent practices
: With every team doing things their way, it becomes challenging to enforce best practices, potentially affecting product quality and security.
Analysis paralysis
: Too many choices can be overwhelming. Instead of speeding up development, it might hinder decision-making.
On the other end of the spectrum, a highly opinionated platform can be stifling:
Resistance to adoption
: Developers might resist using a platform that doesn't accommodate their preferred tools or methods.
Stagnation
: An overly rigid platform can deter innovation. Teams might find it hard to experiment with new technologies or practices.
One-size-fits-none
: While trying to be universally applicable, the platform might end up not being perfectly suited for any particular team or project.
Dependence on platform updates
: Any change or addition might require a full platform update, leading to delays and potential disruptions.
The impact on developer experience and platform adoption
An IDP, regardless of how feature-rich or innovative, will only prove its worth if developers embrace and actively use it. The intersection of flexibility, control, and overall user experience greatly influences platform adoption rates and developers' satisfaction. Let's delve into how to measure this impact and the nuances of choice in platform adoption:
Surveys and questionnaires
: Regularly solicit feedback from developers using structured surveys. This allows for quantitative measurements of satisfaction levels, pain points, and desired features. Tools such as
Net Promoter Score
(
NPS
) can be employed to gauge the likelihood of developers recommending the platform to peers.
Feedback sessions
: Organize open sessions where developers can voice concerns, provide feedback, or even brainstorm about potential improvements. Such interactive platforms not only collect feedback but can also foster a sense of community and involvement.
Platform usage metrics
: Analyze metrics such as frequency of platform usage, duration of sessions, and features most often used. Such data provides insights into which aspects of the platform are most valuable to developers and which might be causing friction.
Incident and support request analysis
: A significant increase in support requests or reported issues can indicate areas of dissatisfaction or confusion. It's essential to continually monitor and address these to enhance the developer experience.
Correlating flexibility with adoption
A common misconception while starting a platform team is that the more flexibility the platform offers, the easier the platform's adoption will be. In most cases, this leads to ad hoc DevOps work, which makes platform teams fall back into their old, fragmented DevOps way of working. To better understand this, we should understand the following concepts:
The paradox of choice
: Contrary to intuition, more choices can sometimes lead to analysis paralysis, where developers are overwhelmed by options. While it might seem that offering numerous tools and pathways would cater to every developer's needs, it can instead cause confusion or inertia, slowing down the decision-making process.
Standardization versus innovation
: While providing flexibility can spur innovation, there's also value in standardization, especially when onboarding new team members or ensuring consistent delivery across teams. A harmonized toolset and process can significantly speed up ramp-up times and lead to predictable results.
Tailoring to the majority
: By analyzing the preferences and needs of the majority of developers, organizations can strike a balance in their IDP offerings. While it's essential to cater to niche requirements, optimizing the platform for the majority can drive higher overall adoption rates.
Feedback loops
: Regularly reassess the correlation between platform flexibility and adoption. By maintaining open channels of communication with developers and monitoring platform usage trends, organizations can make iterative adjustments to better align with developers' evolving needs.
Crafting reusable solutions for flexibility over time
The technology landscape is ever-evolving, and what works today might not necessarily be the best fit tomorrow. Moreover, as an organization grows and matures, its technological requirements will inevitably shift. Therefore, while an IDP's initial setup is crucial, its ability to adapt and improve over time is just as – if not more – important. This section delves into the nuances of evolving an IDP to maintain its relevance and effectiveness. To successfully grow into platform engineering but also grow and evolve your platform practices, you should have the following:
Feedback channels
: Regular feedback mechanisms can be invaluable in understanding the changing needs of the developer community. These channels can be formal, such as periodic surveys or focus groups, or informal, such as open forums or chat groups dedicated to platform feedback.
Performance metrics
: Monitor platform usage metrics, latency, error rates, and other KPIs that can provide insights into areas that might need improvements.
External indicators
: Stay abreast of industry trends, emerging technologies, and best practices. These can act as indicators for potential areas of improvement or incorporation into the platform.
Product life cycle evaluation
: As the platform matures, certain components might become obsolete or less relevant. Regularly evaluate the life cycle of each component while considering factors such as vendor support, community engagement, and alignment with the organization's strategic goals.
A very important concept for building reusable tools is that of Golden Paths or Paved Roads.
Building golden paths
Guiding developers through a labyrinth of choices and ensuring consistency, speed, and quality is a challenge. One solution that has gained traction among leading tech companies is the concept of
golden paths
. These paths provide developers with a set of best practices, tools, and frameworks optimized for the specific environment of an organization.
Understanding golden paths
Golden paths
, as conceived by companies such as Red Hat and VMware, represent the convergence of experience and optimization. For these organizations, the golden paths are essentially the most efficient, secure, and scalable ways to achieve specific tasks or outcomes. They are the result of rigorous testing, feedback, and iteration, and they encapsulate the collective knowledge of the organization.Spotify takes a slightly different approach but with the same core idea. They term it
paved roads
. For Spotify, these are the paths of least resistance and are where developers find everything they need to get their job done quickly and efficiently. When a developer follows a paved road, they're supported by the platform, and everything
just works
. But, if they decide to go off the paved road, they might face more challenges and get less support.The establishment of golden paths has several key impacts on development:
Consistency and standardization
: Golden paths offer a standardized approach to common challenges. When developers follow these paths, the resulting solutions are consistent in design, architecture, and even coding patterns, which simplifies maintenance and troubleshooting.
Accelerated development
: Since golden paths provide proven solutions to common problems, developers don't have to
reinvent the wheel
every time. This can considerably cut down the time spent on R&D, testing, and troubleshooting.
Knowledge transfer
: For new team members, golden paths act as a primer for the organization's best practices. They can quickly come up to speed by following the laid-out paths and understanding both the
how
and the
why
behind certain decisions.
Golden paths, or paved roads, are powerful tools for organizations aiming to harmonize their development processes. While they come with undeniable benefits, organizations must strike a balance, ensuring that while processes are streamlined, innovation and adaptability are not stifled.
The Golden Path Maturity Model
As with any concept in organizational development and software engineering, the adoption of golden paths isn't a binary process. It's more apt to envision it as a maturity model, where organizations progress through various stages, refining and perfecting their approach to standardization and best practices over time. The
Golden Path Maturity Model
serves as a framework to evaluate the current state of an organization's adoption and provides a roadmap for progression.The Golden Path Maturity Model is designed to categorize organizations based on how they've adopted and integrated the concept of golden paths into their workflows. By determining where they currently stand, organizations can better identify gaps, create actionable plans, and achieve a more streamlined, efficient, and consistent development process.The following are the three different levels of maturity:
No golden path
(
Teams have their own DevOps practices
):
At this initial stage, there is little to no standardization across teams. Each team operates in silos, developing and deploying using their own methodologies and toolsets.
Pros
: Maximum flexibility; teams can choose technologies and processes tailored to their unique needs.
Cons
: Results in duplicated effort, increased complexity in collaboration, and a higher potential for errors.
Clone and forget
(
Centrally provided tools, but not a reusable platform yet
):
This is a transitional phase. Here, central teams provide solutions and tools that can be copied and adapted by individual teams. Templates to promote best practices or small automation tools are usually found at this stage.
Pros
: Introduces a level of consistency, reduces the diversity of tools, and begins to establish best practices.
Cons
: While tools and solutions are available, they are often used inconsistently. There may also be a lack of holistic integration as these solutions are not yet unified into a cohesive platform.
Golden paths as products
(
This is your IDP
):
This is the pinnacle of the maturity model. Golden paths are no longer just guidelines but are integrated as first-class entities within an IDP.
Pros
: Maximum efficiency, consistency, and scalability. Best practices are not only suggested but baked into the platform itself, streamlining development significantly.
Cons
: Requires a more considerable upfront investment in setting up, refining, and maintaining the platform.
Throughout the DevOps journey, the maturity of golden paths should progress in parallel. As teams become more agile, integrated, and automated, the golden paths should adapt to provide the most value, driving efficiency, consistency, and quality in the software development life cycle.Golden paths, when approached with the understanding of their maturity model, can serve as invaluable tools for organizations looking to drive excellence in their software development processes. By knowing where they stand and where they need to go, organizations can chart a clear course toward development efficiency and consistency.
Implementing golden paths
Golden paths represent the intersection of best practices and efficiency, guiding development teams toward optimal solutions while maintaining a level of flexibility. This section explores the systematic implementation of golden paths within an organization.Let's consider the development of a golden path for each maturity level:
No Golden Path level
:
Focus
: Start by identifying glaring inconsistencies and redundancies across teams.
Implementation
: Design a baseline golden path that introduces minimal standardization, targeting the most common tasks or tools. At this stage, you focus on the
why
, describing what outcome or goal you want to achieve with a solution or automation. With this, each team can work on creating the tools to achieve that on their own.
Clone and forget level
:
Focus
: At this level, the emphasis should be on expanding the golden path concepts to encompass more workflows and tools, but with flexibility.
Implementation
: Develop centrally-provided tools or templates that can be cloned and adapted by teams. Introduce basic integration mechanisms to ensure smoother workflows.
Golden paths as products level
:
Focus
: The focus here is on deep integration, where golden paths are fully incorporated into the IDP.
Implementation
: Design holistic solutions that unify disparate tools and processes under the umbrella of the IDP. Golden paths should be seamlessly integrated, providing an out-of-the-box solution for the most frequent development tasks. At this point, a platform is provided that covers the key capabilities needed, and a self-service onboarding method is in place to reduce friction.
Examples of golden paths
Golden paths, as best practice guides, can cover a myriad of aspects within the development ecosystem. Here, we'll delve into specific examples encompassing pipelines, observability, and deploying new applications, and how each can evolve across the maturity model.
Pipelines (CI/CD)
A common starting point for many platform teams is standardizing build and release pipelines. There are a few steps until a platform team can reach a Pipeline-as-as-Product approach:
No golden path level
: At this stage, teams might have different CI/CD processes or even lack some entirely. Pipelines could be manual, or inconsistently automated, leading to potential delays and errors.
Clone and forget level
: A central CI/CD template is provided for teams to clone and adapt. The pipeline would have stages for building, testing, and deploying, but specifics would be left for individual teams to customize based on their needs.
Golden paths as products level
: The CI/CD pipeline is a well-integrated, fully featured solution. It offers advanced features such as parallel builds, canary deployments, and rollback mechanisms. Standardized yet customizable stages ensure uniformity while allowing for unique project needs.

Observability
Observability is also one of the engineering functions where we see the most diversity in terms of tools and implementation across different teams, and naturally, it is one of the usual early starting points of IDPs:
No golden path level
: Teams may use different tools and standards for logging, monitoring, and alerting. Some critical metrics or logs might be missed, and there could be redundancies in others.
Clone and forget level
: A basic observability template provides guidelines on what metrics to monitor, how to log effectively, and when to trigger alerts. However, teams might still choose different tools or slightly modify these guidelines based on their preferences.
Golden paths as products level
: A unified observability platform is provided, with integrations to all major tools and services used within the organization. This platform will prescribe best practices, such as setting up dashboards, monitoring key performance indicators, and setting alerts based on SLOs. The data collected is consistent across teams, facilitating easier cross-team troubleshooting and analysis.
Deploying new applications
Platform teams are designed to help organizations scale, so besides modernizing existing tools and services, IDPs should enable easier development and deployment of new greenfield projects:
No golden path level
: Deploying a new application could be a tedious process, with manual configurations, unchecked dependencies, and potential for errors. There might not be a standardized way to handle environment variables, secrets, or database migrations.
Clone and forget level
: A central repository or wiki provides templates and guidelines for deploying new applications. It would cover aspects such as containerization, environment setup, and database initialization. Teams can take these guidelines and adapt them based on the specifics of their application or the infrastructure they're deploying to.
Golden paths as products level
: The IDP provides a seamless creation experience. The platform provides a template repository, scaffolding features, and integration with different tools. Pre-configured templates for various types of applications (web applications, microservices, and batch jobs) ensure that applications are deployed using best practices consistently.
By progressing through these stages, organizations gradually eliminate inefficiencies and uncertainties. The result is a streamlined, predictable, and efficient development and deployment process that reaps the benefits in terms of time, cost, and product quality.
Transitioning to platform
The shift to a platform-centric model from traditional development approaches or scattered, tool-based environments is both strategic and intricate. The underlying motivation for such a transition often springs from a desire for streamlined processes, enhanced collaboration, and an aspiration to be at par with the best in the industry. However, convincing stakeholders, addressing concerns, and navigating challenges are crucial to ensure a smooth transition. One of the very important early decisions of a platform team is what to build and what to use as a third-party procured service.
Understanding the platform transition
One of the main drivers behind the shift to a platform model is scalability. Organizations, as they burgeon, often find that linearly scaling their infrastructure and development processes is not just cumbersome but also inefficient. A platform provides a more holistic solution, offering an avenue for seamlessly onboarding new teams or projects. This comes hand in hand with consistency. By adopting a platform-centric approach, companies can ensure that there are standardized tools, processes, and guidelines in place. This not only ensures consistent development and deployment practices across various teams but also reduces the chances of errors. Additionally, the inherent automation capabilities of platforms can markedly accelerate product development cycles by reducing manual touchpoints. And beyond just the technical advantages, platforms can be a catalyst for fostering a more collaborative organizational culture. This unity in approach and being able to share resources and best practices can break down the silos that often hinder innovation.
Convincing leadership
Transitioning to a platform is not a minor undertaking and getting leadership on board is pivotal. When presenting the case to the upper echelons of management, it's beneficial to underscore the long-term cost savings. This can be attributed to reduced overhead, the potential for tool consolidation, and the ability to bring products to market at a faster clip. Moreover, in today's hyper-competitive business landscape, having a platform can offer a tangible strategic advantage. It not only facilitates rapid adaptability to market changes but can also position the company as a vanguard in its domain. On the risk front, the consistency that platforms bring to the table can be a significant risk mitigator. With standardized environments and processes, outcomes become more predictable. Plus, in the ever-evolving technological landscape, being platform-centric ensures the organization remains agile and future-ready.
Convincing teams
While leadership buy-in is crucial, the teams – the people who will be working with the platform day in, day out – need to be convinced too. It's essential to illustrate the tangible benefits they stand to gain. This could be in the form of superior integrated tooling that the platform offers, or the potential reduction in repetitive tasks thanks to automation. By removing mundane tasks from their plate, teams can pivot their focus more toward innovation and core development activities. Furthermore, platforms often come embedded with industry best practices. This means that teams have a perpetual opportunity to work with state-of-the-art technologies and methodologies, enhancing their own skill sets in the process. And finally, the promise of a more collaborative work environment can be a significant draw. Platforms inherently promote collective problem-solving by eroding the barriers that exist in more siloed work setups.Of course, the path to platform transition is not without its challenges. It's common to encounter resistance, especially from teams that are deeply entrenched in a certain way of working. They may view this shift as a disruption. Technical challenges can also crop up, especially when trying to integrate existing tools or processes into the new platform. In some cases, there may be a discernible skill gap, necessitating either hiring new talent or upskilling current employees. And, from an economic standpoint, the initial phases of the transition – encompassing setup, migration, and training – can bear significant cost implications.
Managing the transition process
The crux of any technological transition doesn't rest solely on the shoulders of the technology itself, but rather on the individuals who utilize it daily. Legacy tools and systems often come with a deep sense of ownership and familiarity for teams. Convincing these teams to move away requires a delicate balance of rational argument, empathetic understanding, and tangible incentives:
Recognizing emotional attachments
: Legacy tools are more than just code or interfaces; they are repositories of memories, hard work, and countless hours of debugging. Acknowledging this emotional tie is the first step toward initiating a dialog about transition.
Showcasing tangible benefits
: A theoretical argument about efficiency may not resonate as powerfully as a hands-on demonstration. Arrange for workshops or pilot programs where teams can see the advantages of platform solutions in terms of speed, ease, and potential outcomes firsthand.
Incentivizing the transition
: Offering training programs, certifications, or even gamified challenges related to the platform can turn the transition into an engaging and rewarding journey. This not only helps in skill-building but also fosters a sense of achievement among members.
Open feedback channels
: An open-door policy, where teams can voice their concerns, reservations, or even nostalgic recounts of their experiences with older tools, can be invaluable. It builds trust and paves the way for constructive discussions.
Collaboration is the bedrock upon which successful platform transitions are built. As the move toward a platform-centric approach gains momentum, the nature of collaboration changes. It's not just about working alongside but working intertwined, with clear goals and collective responsibilities.The structure of the platform team becomes crucial in this environment. While detailed insights on this are reserved for
Chapter 7
, it's important to highlight a few critical aspects here:
Diverse skill sets
: A platform team shouldn't be monolithic in its expertise. Ideally, it should comprise members with varied skill sets, from infrastructure experts to UI/UX designers, ensuring a holistic approach to platform design and development.
Clearly defined roles
: As collaboration intensifies, role clarity ensures there's no overlap or ambiguity. Whether it's the platform architect, the security expert, or the integration specialist, each role should have a clear set of responsibilities and deliverables.
Promoting inter-team dialogs
: Encourage regular interactions between the platform team and other teams. Such interactions are an opportunity to exchange feedback, share success stories, and jointly troubleshoot challenges.
Iterative development approach
: Platforms evolve, and so should the team's approach. Adopting an iterative mindset, where feedback from internal users (other teams) is actively sought and incorporated, ensures the platform remains relevant and user-centric.
Building a platform is a transformative endeavor, not just in terms of technology but also in organizational culture. It demands a departure from siloed operations and a move toward a more cohesive, collaborative future. With the right team structure and motivation strategies, this journey can lead to unparalleled efficiency and innovation.

Introduction to Cloud Native Operational Excellence
Cloud Native Operational Excellence
(
CNOE
) represents a significant shift in how enterprises approach their cloud platform strategies, especially when adopting
Open Source Software
(
OSS
) as the foundation. This approach is designed to address the complexities and challenges associated with selecting and implementing technologies in a cloud-native landscape, where the choices made can have far-reaching implications for the next 3-5 years.CNOE is not just about creating a unified control plane or a set of CI/CD tools; it's a broader framework for integrating various components and capabilities that enhance application integration and delivery. It's not about introducing new technologies or managed services but providing a methodology to interact and integrate existing tools effectively. CNOE is fully open-source, encouraging customization and broad usability, and it's not responsible for operationalizing the toolchain, meaning enterprises still need to fund and manage their open-source tools within the IDP.Enterprises face the daunting task of choosing technologies that will not only support their current operational needs but also remain relevant and efficient in the future. The CNOE framework addresses this by bringing together enterprises operating at similar scales to collaboratively navigate their operational technology decisions, thereby de-risking their tooling investments and coordinating contributions.Key challenges that CNOE addresses include the following:
The Paralysis of Choice
: The vast CNCF landscape and the proliferation of tools often leave enterprises overwhelmed, leading to a lack of consensus and maintainability challenges.
Evolving Definitions of CI/CD
: Traditional systems are becoming outdated, making way for more modern tools like ArgoCD, Flux, and Tekton that embrace paradigms like GitOps.
Declarative, Centralized Control Planes
: The rise of Kubernetes has created an ecosystem that is fundamentally different from previous generations, necessitating a shift in tooling and practices.
Developer Workflow Discontinuity
: Despite technological advancements, core developer workflows have remained largely unchanged, leading to a gap between development and delivery.
Fragmentation in Software Delivery
: The multitude of languages, IaC platforms, and packaging systems has led to non-portable software components, making it challenging to consolidate into a unified application.
Key tenants
CNOE operates based on the following key tenets:
Open Source First
: Prioritizing open-source technology for technology verticals to ensure alignment and collaborative freedom.
Community-Driven
: Direction and decisions are driven by the community and its governing body, focusing on technology selection and contribution levels.
Tools, Not Practices
: CNOE suggests tools and configurations, leaving practices up to the individual companies.

Kubeadm - a project to simplify Kubernetes cluster management
kubeadm
is a tool developed to simplify the process of setting up and managing a Kubernetes cluster. It's designed as part of the Kubernetes project and provides a straightforward way to get a secure and up-to-date cluster up and running in a more manageable way compared to manual setup.The Kubeadm process involves the following steps:
Install Prerequisites on All Nodes
: This includes setting up a container runtime and ensuring network configurations are correct. Prerequisites also involve installing
kubeadm
,
kubelet
, and
kubectl
on each node.
Initialize the Master Node with kubeadm init
: This command sets up the Kubernetes control plane on the master node. Under the hood, it runs several tasks such as generating necessary certificates for secure communication, setting up the Kubernetes API server, and configuring the
etcd
datastore.
A single command to setup the master node:
kubeadm init --pod-network-cidr=10.244.0.0/16
Set Up Worker Nodes with kubeadm join
: Once the master is initialized, worker nodes can be added to the cluster using the
kubeadm join
command. This command requires a token and CA certificate hash provided by the initialization output of the master node.
A single command to setup and join the worker nodes:
kubeadm join [API_SERVER_IP]:6443 --token [TOKEN] --discovery-token-ca-cert-hash sha256:[HASH]
Running those commands includes multiple tasks for each. What happens under the Hood?:
kubeadm init
: This command starts by running pre-flight checks to ensure the node can run Kubernetes. It then creates a unique token for node registration, sets up the necessary certificates, and configures the control plane components.
kubeadm join
: It communicates with the specified master, verifies the provided token, and configures the node to join the cluster as a worker, setting up necessary Kubernetes components.
kubeadm greatly streamlines the Kubernetes deployment process, making it accessible to a wider range of users, from beginners to experienced system administrators. By abstracting complex tasks into simple commands, it reduces the barrier to entry for managing Kubernetes clusters.
How many clusters do I need?
In Kubernetes' early adoption phase, many organizations opted for a single, large cluster. This approach stemmed from the desire to simplify management and maximize resource utilization. However, several challenges and limitations soon became apparent:
Scalability and Performance Issues
: Large clusters often faced difficulties in scaling efficiently, especially as the number of nodes and pods increased. This led to challenges in scheduling, network traffic management, and overall cluster performance.
Risk Concentration
: A single cluster meant that all applications shared the same underlying infrastructure. This created a single point of failure, where issues in one part of the cluster could potentially impact the entire system, increasing the risk of widespread outages.
Complexity in Security and Compliance
: Managing security and compliance in a large, monolithic cluster was challenging. Implementing and enforcing varied security policies across different applications within the same cluster was not only complex but also posed significant risks.
Resource Contention
: In a single-cluster setup, different applications often competed for the same resources, leading to contention and inefficient resource utilization. This made it difficult to guarantee the performance and availability of critical applications.
To address these challenges, the trend shifted towards deploying multiple smaller clusters. This approach aligns better with the principles of cloud-native architecture. The decision between one cluster per application versus one cluster with all applications per environment hinges on several factors:
Isolation
: One cluster per application offers maximum isolation, reducing the risk of cross-application interference and improving security. However, it can increase management overhead and complexity.
Cost Efficiency
: While multiple clusters provide better isolation and resource allocation, they can also increase operational costs. Balancing cost with operational needs is crucial in deciding the right approach.
Resource Management
: For organizations with diverse application portfolios, multiple clusters can offer more flexible and efficient resource management, tailored to the specific needs of each application.
Security and Compliance
: Different applications may have different security and compliance requirements. Multiple clusters allow for more tailored security management, aligning with specific regulatory and organizational standards.
Technical considerations, such as network latency, cluster capacity, and operational complexity, play a significant role in deciding the number of clusters.

Patterns of multi-cluster Kubernetes
Kubernetes plays a pivotal role in enabling multi-cloud strategies for organizations. It acts as a bridge between different cloud providers, providing a consistent layer of abstraction over diverse cloud infrastructures. This abstraction allows development teams to focus on building and deploying applications without needing to worry about the underlying cloud-specific details.In a multi-cloud environment, Kubernetes facilitates seamless migration and management of applications. It offers a unified set of APIs and tools across clouds, which simplifies the process of deploying and managing applications in a heterogeneous cloud environment.Effective strategies for multi-cloud deployment with Kubernetes include using cluster federation to manage multiple clusters across different clouds, implementing centralized logging and monitoring for visibility across clouds, using consistent CI/CD pipelines for deployment automation, and leveraging centralized authentication and authorization to incorporate enterprise identity management.Exploring multi-cluster Kubernetes architectures, particularly cluster federation, involves understanding how to manage multiple Kubernetes clusters as a single entity. Cluster federation allows for the synchronization of configurations, policies, and deployments across various clusters, regardless of their location. This architecture is especially useful in scenarios requiring high availability, disaster recovery, and global load balancing. By federating clusters, organizations can spread their workloads across different regions or cloud providers, enhancing resilience and optimizing resource usage. The federation also simplifies the management of common resources and policies across these clusters, making it an efficient strategy for handling complex, multi-cluster environments.The hub-spoke architecture in multi-cluster Kubernetes environments is a strategic approach to managing multiple clusters. In this model, a central
hub
cluster oversees multiple
spoke
clusters. The hub serves as the administrative control point, from where global policies, configurations, and monitoring are managed. Each spoke cluster operates as an independent entity but is linked to the hub for centralized governance and oversight. This architecture simplifies the management of multi-cluster environments, allowing for consistent policy enforcement and a unified view of cluster health and performance across different cloud providers or regions. The hub-spoke model enhances operational efficiency and is especially effective in scenarios requiring centralized control with decentralized operation.Cluster replication in multi-cluster Kubernetes architectures refers to a strategy where one or more clusters are replicated across different environments, usually for the purposes of high availability, disaster recovery, or geographic distribution. This approach involves synchronizing the state and configuration of one cluster (the primary) to other clusters (replicas). Replication can be used to ensure that if one cluster fails or becomes unreachable, its workload can be rapidly shifted to or taken over by another cluster. This model enhances resilience and ensures continuous operation, which is vital for mission-critical applications. Cluster replication is particularly useful in scenarios where consistent performance across geographically distributed regions is required.

Extending Kubernetes
Kubernetes, as a robust and flexible platform, offers extensive capabilities for managing containerized applications. However, to tailor Kubernetes to meet specific organizational requirements, understanding and leveraging its extensibility is crucial. This involves a deep dive into the Kubernetes Controller Manager, the control loop mechanism, and the implementation of
Custom Resource Definitions
(
CRDs
) and custom controllers.At the core of Kubernetes' ability to manage and maintain the desired state of applications and resources is the Kubernetes Controller Manager. This component runs various controllers, each responsible for specific aspects of cluster management.The fundamental operation of these controllers is based on a
control loop mechanism
. This loop continually watches the state of your cluster through the Kubernetes API, compares the current state with the desired state, and takes action to reconcile any differences. The process involves three primary steps:
Observe
: The controller observes the current state of the cluster or a particular resource.
Analyze
: It compares this observed state to the desired state defined by the user or default system settings.
Act
: If discrepancies are found, the controller takes action to bring the current state in line with the desired state.
This continuous monitoring and automatic correction mechanism is what grants Kubernetes its self-healing and auto-scaling capabilities.To extend Kubernetes beyond its built-in resource types, CRDs and custom controllers are used. These tools allow users to define and manage new, custom resources that are native to Kubernetes.
Implementing Custom Resource Definitions
CRDs allow you to define new types of resources that behave like built-in Kubernetes objects. This includes defining the name, version, and schema of the new resources. A CRD defines how these new objects will look and what features they will have. Once defined, these custom resources can be used like any standard Kubernetes object. You can create, update, and delete these resources using kubectl or other Kubernetes API clients.Alongside CRDs, custom controllers are necessary to imbue these new resources with functionality. These controllers use the same control loop mechanism as built-in controllers to watch the custom resources and ensure the correct state. Creating a custom controller in Kubernetes can be an intricate task, but tools like Kubebuilder simplify the process. Kubernetes Operators represent a paradigm shift in the way applications are managed in Kubernetes environments. They extend Kubernetes' capabilities, allowing for more automated and efficient management of complex, stateful applications. This introduction to Kubernetes Operators will explore what they are, how they work, and their significance in modern cloud-native application management.According to RedHat
"Operators are a method of packaging, deploying, and managing a Kubernetes application. A Kubernetes application is an application that is both deployed on Kubernetes and managed using the Kubernetes APIs and kubectl tooling."
The key idea behind Operators is to encode the operational knowledge of experienced administrators into software. This allows for automating complex tasks that would traditionally require manual intervention. In simple terms, an operator is designed to detect changes in a custom resource, and use the Kubernetes API to adjust the current state to meet the desired state (
Figure 6.5
)
Figure 6.5 – How does an operator work
Operators are implemented as a set of application-specific controllers deployed on Kubernetes. These controllers extend the Kubernetes API and manage the lifecycle of specific resources related to the application. They watch for changes to objects related to their application, then take the necessary actions to reach the desired state.The core components of an Operator include:
CRDs
: CRDs extend the Kubernetes API to introduce new resource types. These custom resources represent the application and its components.
Custom controllers
: These are the brains of the Operator. They watch for changes to the custom resources and other Kubernetes objects and take actions to align the current state of the system with the desired state.
Operational Logic
: This is encoded into the Operator and represents the human knowledge required to manage the application. It includes handling updates, backups, failovers, scaling, and other operational tasks.
Operators offer several key benefits in managing applications within Kubernetes:
Automated Application Management
: They automate complex operational tasks, reducing the need for manual intervention and enabling more consistent and reliable operations.
Deeper Insights and Control
: Operators provide detailed insights into the application's state and behavior and allow for more granular control over its lifecycle.
Easier Deployment and Scalability
: Operators make deploying and scaling applications more straightforward and consistent across different environments.
Customization and Extensibility
: Operators can be tailored to the specific needs of an application, providing custom functionality and workflows.
Operators are particularly beneficial in scenarios where applications require complex setup, configuration, and maintenance. Common use cases include:
Stateful Applications
: Managing databases, message brokers, and other stateful services that require persistent storage and complex lifecycle management
Complex Deployments
: Applications that require complex installation and update processes can benefit from the automation provided by Operators
Multi-Tenant Systems
: Operators can manage resources and isolation in multi-tenant environments effectively
Auto-Remediation
: Automatically handling failovers, backups, and recovery processes
Kubernetes Operators are a powerful tool in the arsenal of cloud-native application management. Managing infrastructure in a Kubernetes-native way
As Kubernetes evolves, so does its ability to manage resources beyond just containers. The concept of treating everything as Kubernetes resources, extended to external resources through Kubernetes Operators, has gained significant traction. This approach offers a Kubernetes-native way of handling a wide range of infrastructure components, from databases and storage systems to complete cloud service environments.The core idea here is to manage external resources using the same Kubernetes API and tooling that developers are already familiar with. This is achieved through Kubernetes Operators that are specifically designed to handle external resources.Kubernetes Operators extend the Kubernetes API to create, configure, and manage instances of complex stateful applications on behalf of a Kubernetes user. They encapsulate operational knowledge and can automate the management of external resources just as they do for in-cluster resources.For example, the AWS Service Operator allows you to create AWS resources directly from Kubernetes. This means you can define an Amazon S3 bucket or an Amazon RDS instance as a Kubernetes resource, and the operator will handle the provisioning and management of these resources in AWS, mirroring the state defined in your Kubernetes manifests.The following are the advantages of this approach:
Unified Management Interface
: Managing external resources through Kubernetes Operators allows for a consistent and unified management interface. This reduces the complexity of dealing with multiple APIs and interfaces.
Declarative Configuration
: Like other Kubernetes resources, external resources are managed declaratively. This means the desired state of these resources is defined in YAML manifests, which the operator works to achieve and maintain.
Improved Developer Productivity
: Developers can provision and manage necessary infrastructure directly from Kubernetes without needing deep expertise in the underlying platforms.
Automation and Orchestration
: Operators automate provisioning, updates, and management tasks, which can significantly reduce the operational overhead and potential for human error.
Seamless Integration
: This approach allows for seamless integration of cloud services and other external resources with the Kubernetes ecosystem, enhancing the overall cloud-native experience.

) have emerged as powerful tools for managing Kubernetes clusters at scale.CAPI is a Kubernetes project that brings declarative, Kubernetes-style APIs to cluster creation, configuration, and management. It provides a consistent and repeatable way to manage clusters, including their lifecycle, configuration, and upgrades.The key features of CAPI are the following:
Declarative API for Cluster Management
: Just like managing applications within Kubernetes, CAPI allows you to manage clusters using a declarative API. You define your cluster specification, and Cluster API takes care of the rest.
Multi-Cloud and Hybrid-Cloud Support
: CAPI is designed to work across different cloud providers and on-premises environments, making it a versatile tool for diverse infrastructures.
Automated Lifecycle Management
: It automates the tasks associated with managing cluster lifecycles, such as creation, scaling, upgrading, and deletion.
Extensibility and Customization
: Being part of the Kubernetes ecosystem, it allows for customization and extensibility in line with other Kubernetes resources.
Deploying a Kubernetes cluster using the CAPI involves several steps, starting from installing the CAPI components in your existing Kubernetes cluster (often referred to as the management or hub cluster) to creating and managing workload clusters. Here's a detailed step-by-step guide.The following are the prerequisites for the same:
Management Cluster
: A pre-existing Kubernetes cluster where the Cluster API components will be deployed. This cluster is used to manage workload clusters. This is our Hub or Central cluster
kubectl
: Installed and configured to communicate with your management cluster.
Helm or Kustomize
: For installing Cluster API components.
The following are the steps for the process:
Install Cluster API Components
: Depending on your preference, you can use Helm or Kustomize to install the Cluster API components. If using Helm:
helm repo add cluster-api https://cluster-api.sigs.k8s.io/charts
helm repo update
helm install cluster-api \
cluster-api/cluster-api \
--namespace capi-system \
--create-namespace
Install Infrastructure Provider
: Cluster API uses infrastructure providers to interact with different cloud providers or environments. For instance, to install the AWS infrastructure provider, use:
kubectl apply -f \
https://github.com/kubernetes-sigs/cluster-api-provider-aws/ \
releases/download/v0.6.4/infrastructure-components.yaml
Create Cluster Configuration
: Use
clusterctl
to generate cluster configuration templates. For AWS, set up environment variables for your AWS credentials and then run:
clusterctl generate cluster capi-quickstart \
--flavor development \
--kubernetes-version v1.21.2 \
--control-plane-machine-count=3 \
--worker-machine-count=3 > capi-quickstart.yaml
This generates a YAML file with the configuration for your new cluster.
Deploy Workload Cluster
: Deploy the workload cluster by applying the generated configuration.
kubectl apply -f capi-quickstart.yaml
Access the Workload Cluster
:
Retrieve Kubeconfig
: Once the workload cluster is up, you can get its
kubeconfig
file:
kubectl --namespace=default get secret/capi-quickstart-kubeconfig \
-o jsonpath={.data.value} | base64 --decode \
> ./capi-quickstart.kubeconfig
Access the Cluster
: Use the retrieved
kubeconfig
file to interact with your new cluster:
kubectl --kubeconfig=./capi-quickstart.kubeconfig get nodes
In our
capi-quickstart.yaml
file we define multiple Custom Resources:
Cluster Definition
: This section defines the cluster and its network settings, including CIDR blocks for services and pods.
AWSCluster
: Specifies AWS-specific configuration like the region and SSH key name for cluster instances.
MachineDeployment
: Defines a set of worker nodes, their Kubernetes version, and references to bootstrap and infrastructure templates.
AWSMachineTemplate
: Specifies AWS-specific settings for the worker nodes, such as the instance type and IAM instance profile.
apiVersion: cluster.x-k8s.io/v1alpha4
kind: Cluster
metadata:
  name: capi-quickstart
  namespace: default
spec:
  clusterNetwork:
    services:
      cidrBlocks: ["10.128.0.0/12"]
    pods:
      cidrBlocks: ["192.168.0.0/16"]
    serviceDomain: "cluster.local"
  controlPlaneEndpoint:
    host: "control-plane.example.com"
    port: 6443
  infrastructureRef:
    apiVersion: infrastructure.cluster.x-k8s.io/v1alpha4
    kind: AWSCluster
    name: capi-quickstart

  name: capi-quickstart

  name: capi-quickstart-md-0

  clusterName: capi-quickstart
  replicas: 3

    matchLabels: {}

    metadata:
      labels: {}

      clusterName: capi-quickstart

        configRef:
          apiVersion: bootstrap.cluster.x-k8s.io/v1alpha4

        apiVersion: infrastructure.cluster.x-k8s.io/v1alpha4

      sshKeyName: default
Under the hood, CAPI uses the kubeadm project to bootstrap cluster nodes.
