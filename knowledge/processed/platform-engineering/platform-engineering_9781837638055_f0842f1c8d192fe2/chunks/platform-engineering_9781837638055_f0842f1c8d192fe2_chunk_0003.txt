Conway's Law and Fit-for-Purpose teams
In 1968, Melvin Conway, an early pioneer of software development, made an astute observation about the way organizations develop software. This observation, now known as Conway's Law, posits: "
Any organization that designs a system (defined broadly) will produce a design whose structure is a copy of the organization's communication structure.
"In simpler terms, Conway's Law suggests that the way teams are organized in a company will invariably influence the design of the products they create. This principle has profound implications for software development and platform engineering, as it provides a lens through which we can better understand the often complex relationship between an organization's structure and the systems it produces.Conway's Law has been validated by multiple studies and observed in action in numerous organizations. The law is particularly relevant to the modern world of microservices and distributed systems, where architectural boundaries often mirror team boundaries. For instance, if a company has a team dedicated to user authentication, it is likely that the company's software will have a distinct user authentication service.

Organizational design for large scale engineering teams
In the world of microservices and cloud-native technologies, aligning organizational design with technical architecture is crucial. This creates a clear understanding of areas of ownership, encourages loose coupling of teams (and therefore services), and fosters innovation within those areas of responsibility. Leveraging Conway's Law in organizing a platform engineering team
When it comes to structuring a platform engineering team, Conway's Law can prove to be a potent tool. The relationship between the structure of an organization and its produced designs becomes evident when you examine the output produced by different team arrangements.Consider, for instance, a scenario where each product team has an embedded DevOps engineer or a DevOps team. With this setup, the most likely outcome is a series of automation solutions that are specific to each product. This is because the DevOps efforts are closely aligned with the specific needs and processes of each product team, resulting in highly tailored solutions. Although this model promotes close collaboration and can be highly effective, it risks creating silos and duplicating effort. Each product team could end up solving the same problems independently and in slightly different ways, reducing the potential for shared learnings and efficiencies.In contrast, consider a setup where there is a dedicated platform engineering team. This team's objective is to build the foundation upon which all products are built, a shared platform that serves the needs of multiple product teams. With Conway's Law in mind, the design of this platform would likely reflect the structure and communication patterns of the platform team. The platform would be a coherent, unified product, instead of a collection of disparate solutions. This doesn't mean that the platform can't be flexible and cater to specific product needs, but rather that there's a consistent underlying infrastructure that can be leveraged across the organization.Despite the advantages, leveraging Conway's Law in this way is not without challenges. For one, the platform engineering team must have a broad and deep understanding of the needs and processes of the product teams it serves. This is crucial to ensure the platform delivers value and is aligned with the product teams' needs. The platform team must also strike a balance between creating a platform that's flexible enough to accommodate different product needs, and one that's not too generic or complex to navigate and use.There's also the risk of the platform team becoming a bottleneck in the software delivery process if the platform requires manual of ad-hoc interventions and they can't keep up with the demands of the product teams. To mitigate this risk, some organizations adopt an inner source approach, where product teams can contribute to the platform. This requires a strong culture of collaboration, clear contribution guidelines, and robust testing and integration processes.Another potential solution is to implement a service or product-oriented model for the platform team. This means the team not only builds and maintains the platform but also treats it as a product, focusing on user needs, usability, and continuous improvement. This model encourages the platform team to work closely with the product teams, understand their challenges and needs, and iterate on the platform to better serve its users.

Team topologies
The book "Team topologies" was written in 2019 by Manuel Pais and Matthew Skelton and is probably one of the most impactful writings in understanding Platform teams, why they exist, and how they interact with other teams in an organization. Team topologies examines the arrangement or pattern of teams in an organization, and it analyzes how work is carried out, and how teams communicate and collaborate.The term team topologies provides a well-structured approach to team organization within IT delivery. In their book, Pais and Skelton, propose four fundamental types of teams in software delivery:
Figure 7.4 – Team topologies – the four team types and three interaction models
Stream-aligned teams
: These teams are aligned to a flow of work from a segment of the business domain, usually dedicated to a specific product, service, or feature set. Their goal is to understand and deliver value to the end-user or customer without being obstructed by other teams. They are cross-functional, containing all the necessary skills to achieve their objectives.
Platform teams
: These teams provide a self-service API to other teams. The goal of a platform team is to enable stream-aligned teams to deliver work without needing to understand the underlying complexities. In our context, the platform engineering team would fall into this category.
Enabling teams
: These teams help other teams build up their capabilities and overcome obstacles. They usually have a wide range of skills and act as force multipliers, helping stream-aligned teams to acquire new skills and improve existing ones.
Complicated subsystem teams
: These teams take care of parts of the system that are so intricate or require such specialized knowledge that they can't be effectively handled by a regular stream-aligned team. They are relatively rare in comparison to the other types of teams.
Each of these team topologies plays a unique role, and understanding their individual strengths and purposes is the first step towards organizing a more effective software delivery setup. However, it's important to note that there is no one-size-fits-all answer when it comes to team topology. The most effective setup will depend on the unique requirements, culture, and goals of your organization.Determining the best topology for a platform engineering team involves careful analysis of the organization's needs, culture, and existing technical landscape. The first step is understanding the organization's strategy and objectives. This allows the platform engineering team's purpose and goals to be clearly aligned with the organization's vision.For a platform engineering team, the
platform team

This maps well onto the platform engineering team's mission of enabling faster, more reliable delivery through the provision of an internal developer platform. The team essentially builds and maintains a foundation upon which other teams can work more effectively.However, the dynamic nature of platform engineering often requires it to also fulfill roles typically associated with the
Enabling team
and
Complicated Subsystem team
topologies. As an
Enabling team

, a platform engineering team may handle complex parts of the infrastructure that are critical to the platform's functionality. These parts are often highly specialized (such as HPC infrastructure for scientific computing, or GPU infrastructure for AI model training) and require specific skills and expertise. By centralizing these skills in the platform engineering team, it can streamline the handling of such complex subsystems.The choice of team topology has a significant impact on communication, workflow, and overall performance. A well-structured team can facilitate clear communication, streamline workflows, and improve the overall productivity and effectiveness of the team. Understanding Outputs, Outcomes, Impact
Measuring success in platform engineering efforts is not straightforward. It requires a nuanced understanding of various metrics and their implications. The process begins with differentiating between outputs, outcomes, and impacts—terms often used interchangeably but which hold distinct meanings and significance.A critical aspect of understanding the success of platform engineering is debunking common myths surrounding developer productivity. These myths often mislead organizations and skew their focus away from meaningful metrics.One prevalent myth is that productivity is solely about developer activity. This misconception equates busyness with productivity, ignoring the quality and impact of the work being done. Measuring the number of lines of code written or the number of tasks completed does not necessarily correlate with meaningful progress or value creation.Another myth is that productivity is only about individual performance. In reality, productivity is a collective outcome, heavily influenced by team dynamics, collaboration, and the efficiency of tools and processes in place. Overemphasizing individual metrics can create a competitive rather than collaborative environment, undermining overall team performance.A third myth is that one productivity metric can tell us everything. Relying on a single metric, such as velocity or cycle time, provides a limited view of productivity. Effective measurement requires a combination of metrics that capture different dimensions of productivity, from code quality to delivery speed and user satisfaction.Additionally, there is a belief that productivity measures are useful only for managers. This perspective neglects the value these measures bring to developers themselves. By understanding productivity metrics, developers can identify areas for improvement, streamline their workflows, and achieve greater satisfaction in their work.Lastly, there is the misconception that productivity is only about engineering systems and developer tools. While these elements are crucial, productivity also hinges on organizational culture, communication, and the alignment of technical and business goals.To effectively measure the success of platform engineering, it's essential to understand the progression from output to outcome to impact. This concept, articulated in various management theories, underscores the importance of looking beyond immediate results to long-term value.
Outputs
refer to the direct results of activities, such as the number of features developed or bugs fixed. These are tangible, measurable deliverables produced by the development team. While outputs are necessary for tracking progress, they do not alone indicate whether the work is creating value.
Outcomes
are the changes or benefits resulting from these outputs. They reflect how well the outputs are solving the problems they were intended to address. For example, an outcome might be improved system performance or increased user engagement. Outcomes provide a better measure of effectiveness than outputs, as they consider the real-world application and benefits of the work done.
Impact
goes a step further, evaluating the broader, long-term effects of these outcomes on the business or community. It encompasses the overall value delivered, such as increased revenue, customer satisfaction, or market share. Measuring impact involves assessing how the outcomes contribute to the organization's strategic goals and mission.
Figure 12.1: The relationship between output, outcome, and impact
Understanding this progression helps teams focus on activities that deliver meaningful value. For instance, while it is useful to track the number of deployments (output), it is more valuable to measure the stability and performance of those deployments (outcome) and how they improve customer satisfaction and business metrics (impact).As platform engineering matures, the focus of measurement shifts from outputs to outcomes and ultimately to impact. At the initial stages, measuring success might involve tracking the number of services migrated to the platform or the adoption rate of platform tools (outputs).As the platform matures, the focus should shift to outcomes, such as reducing the time to market for new features, improving deployment frequency, and enhancing system reliability. Ultimately, the goal is to measure the impact, such as increased business agility, higher user satisfaction, and tangible business growth.There is no one-size-fits-all metric or framework for measuring platform engineering success. Each organization's context, goals, and challenges are unique, requiring a tailored approach.However, there are several widely recognized frameworks that provide valuable guidance.
DORA Metrics
, as discussed earlier, focus on software delivery performance, emphasizing metrics such as deployment frequency, lead time for changes, mean time to recovery, and change failure rate. These metrics are useful for assessing the efficiency and reliability of the development and deployment processes.
SPACE Framework
combines leading and lagging indicators to provide a comprehensive view of developer productivity and satisfaction. It includes metrics across five dimensions: Satisfaction and well-being, Performance, Activity, Communication and collaboration, and Efficiency and flow.
DevEx Metrics
focus on developer experience, measuring aspects such as tool usability, workflow efficiency, and the overall satisfaction of developers. These metrics highlight areas that directly impact the daily experiences and productivity of developers.

Understanding and measuring Flow
Flow, a concept rooted in lean and agile methodologies, refers to the smooth and uninterrupted progression of work through a system. In platform engineering, flow signifies the seamless movement of tasks from ideation to deployment, ensuring that work progresses efficiently and without unnecessary delays. The goal is to minimize waste and maximize value delivery, enhancing both developer productivity and the overall performance of the platform.Optimizing flow is crucial for several reasons:
Increased Efficiency
: By reducing bottlenecks and delays, teams can deliver features and fixes more quickly. This leads to faster time-to-market and a more responsive development process.
Higher Quality
: Smooth flow often results in fewer errors and rework, as work is completed more systematically and predictably. This contributes to higher-quality outputs and more reliable software.
Enhanced Developer Experience
: Developers are less likely to experience frustration and burnout when they can work in a well-optimized environment. Improved flow means fewer interruptions, smoother handoffs, and a more satisfying work experience.
Greater Predictability
: With optimized flow, it becomes easier to predict delivery timelines and manage expectations. This predictability is essential for planning and aligning with business goals.
Several metrics help assess and improve flow within platform engineering:
Cycle Time
: This measures the total time taken for a task to move from start to finish. A shorter cycle time indicates more efficient processes and quicker delivery.
Lead Time
: This metric tracks the time from when a feature or fix is requested to when it is delivered to production. It encompasses both development and deployment phases, providing a comprehensive view of efficiency.
Throughput
: The number of tasks completed in a given time frame. Higher throughput suggests better capacity and process efficiency.
Work in Progress
(
WIP
): The number of tasks being worked on at any given time. Managing WIP is crucial to prevent overloading the system and causing delays.
Strategies for Improving Flow
To enhance workflow efficiency and reduce delays, consider implementing the following proven strategies:
Value Stream Mapping
: This technique involves visualizing the entire workflow, identifying each step, and pinpointing areas of waste or delay. By mapping the value stream, teams can better understand the current state and design more efficient processes.
Limiting WIP
: Implementing WIP limits helps prevent bottlenecks and ensures that tasks are completed before new ones are started. This maintains a steady flow and avoids overloading team members.

: CI/CD practices automate and streamline the integration and deployment processes, reducing manual intervention and minimizing delays. This ensures that code changes are tested, integrated, and deployed rapidly and reliably.

Feedback Loops
: Regular feedback loops, such as daily stand-ups and retrospectives, help teams identify issues early, adjust processes, and continuously improve flow. This iterative approach ensures that the team can adapt quickly to changing requirements and challenges.
While optimizing flow is beneficial, it comes with challenges:
Resistance to Change
: Teams may be resistant to adopting new practices and tools, especially if they are used to traditional methods.
Complexity of Workflows
: Complex workflows with multiple dependencies can be difficult to streamline and optimize.
Balancing Speed and Quality
: Striking the right balance between delivering quickly and maintaining high-quality standards can be challenging.
Continuous Monitoring and Adjustment
: Flow optimization is not a one-time effort but requires continuous monitoring, feedback, and adjustment.

Understanding platform metrics
Platform metrics provide insights into the performance, efficiency, and impact of the engineering efforts, enabling teams to make data-driven decisions. Understanding these metrics involves distinguishing between leading and lagging indicators, recognizing the limitations of certain metrics like DORA, and adopting frameworks that offer a comprehensive view, such as SPACE.
Leading indicators
are predictive measures that provide early signs of future performance. These indicators are proactive, helping teams identify potential issues and opportunities for improvement before they impact the end results. An example of a leading indicator can be:
Cycle Time
: The time taken for a task to move from start to finish. Shorter cycle times indicate more efficient processes. Improving Cycle time can be an early indication of improved development lifecycle.
Lagging indicators
, on the other hand, measure the outcomes of past actions. These indicators are reflective, showing the results of previous efforts and providing a historical perspective. In platform engineering, lagging indicators might include:
Mean Time to Recovery (MTTR)
: The average time taken to recover from a failure. Lower MTTR indicates more resilient systems.
Change Failure Rate
: The percentage of changes that result in failures. Lower failure rates indicate higher quality and stability.
Understanding the balance between leading and lagging indicators is crucial for effective performance measurement. While lagging indicators are easier to implement and understand, leading indicators help teams anticipate and prevent issues, driving continuous improvement.

Incident Frequency

Effort and Time Consumption

Security Concerns
: The slow update process impeded the rapid deployment of security patches, raising vulnerabilities.
Error Handling
: Helm’s atomic deployments, while useful, provided generic error messages that were difficult to debug.
CI/CD Limitations
: The distributed nature of the Terraform CI/CD system was not well-suited for deploying and monitoring cluster components.
Visibility Issues
: Monitoring the health of applications across numerous clusters required manual checks, making it inefficient and error-prone.
To address these challenges, Zoominfo identified several critical requirements for a new solution:
Centralized Management
: A centralized solution to efficiently roll out configuration changes to one or more clusters.
Support for Configurations
: The ability to support configuration differences between clusters based on unique requirements.
Risk Management
: A balance between the risk of changes and deployment agility.
Scalability
: A scalable solution that can grow with the infrastructure.
Automation
: Automatic registration and bootstrapping of new clusters without requiring engineer intervention.
Visibility
: Enhanced visibility into the health of specific components across multiple clusters.
Given these requirements, Zoominfo turned to ArgoCD. ArgoCD offers a fully-loaded UI, enabling the deployment of workloads as Helm charts or manifests in a declarative manner while providing comprehensive visibility into workload health.

: The ApplicationSet controller in ArgoCD comprises two main parts - generators and templates. Generators create parameters rendered into the template fields of the ApplicationSet resource. Secrets and Labels

Kubernetes and GitOps at the Edge
Chick-fil-A has adopted Kubernetes to manage its edge computing environment. Each restaurant operates its own Kubernetes cluster, consisting of three small, networked computers with limited hardware specifications. This setup, described as a “micro data center,” allows for local processing of data while ensuring that critical business information is also sent to the cloud for broader analysis.The implementation of GitOps further streamlines this process. GitOps utilizes Git repositories as the single source of truth for infrastructure configurations. By storing configuration files in Git, Chick-fil-A can manage updates and deployments across its numerous Kubernetes clusters through automated pull requests. This ensures consistent and secure updates across all locations without the need for manual intervention.One of the remarkable aspects of Chick-fil-A’s edge computing strategy is its scalability. Each restaurant’s Kubernetes cluster is designed to be easily deployable and self-sustaining. The initial setup involves unboxing the hardware, connecting it to the network, and powering it on. The devices then self-bootstrap and cluster automatically, requiring minimal technical expertise for installation. This simplicity enables Chick-fil-A to rapidly scale its edge computing infrastructure as it opens new locations.Additionally, this decentralized approach allows each restaurant to operate independently, tolerating network failures and ensuring continuous operation even during internet outages. This high availability is critical for maintaining uninterrupted service during peak hours and promotional events​.To maintain visibility and control over its distributed edge computing environment, Chick-fil-A has invested heavily in observability tools. These tools provide real-time monitoring of each Kubernetes cluster, enabling the IT team to detect and address issues promptly. By collecting telemetry data from IoT devices and processing it locally, Chick-fil-A can optimize equipment performance, predict maintenance needs, and enhance overall operational efficiency.The benefits of Chick-fil-A’s edge computing platform are manifold:
Firstly, it significantly reduces latency, as data processing occurs locally rather than relying on centralized cloud servers. This immediate data processing capability is vital for real-time decision-making and enhances the customer experience by ensuring faster service and more efficient operations.
Secondly, the platform’s scalability and ease of deployment allow Chick-fil-A to maintain consistent service standards across all locations. Each restaurant operates as a micro private cloud, seamlessly integrating with the broader network while retaining autonomy over local operations. This hybrid approach ensures that critical business functions continue without interruption, even in the face of network failures or high traffic volumes​.
Lastly, the use of Kubernetes and GitOps provides a robust framework for managing and updating the edge infrastructure. Automated deployments and configurations reduce the risk of human error and ensure that all restaurants are operating with the latest software and security patches. This consistency is crucial for maintaining the integrity and reliability of the entire system.
Chick-fil-A’s edge computing platform exemplifies how traditional businesses can leverage advanced IT solutions to enhance operational efficiency and customer satisfaction. By deploying Kubernetes clusters at each restaurant and utilizing GitOps for automated management, Chick-fil-A has created a resilient, scalable, and efficient infrastructure that meets the demands of modern fast-food operations. As edge computing continues to evolve, Chick-fil-A’s innovative approach serves as a model for other enterprises looking to harness the power of local data processing and real-time analytics.
