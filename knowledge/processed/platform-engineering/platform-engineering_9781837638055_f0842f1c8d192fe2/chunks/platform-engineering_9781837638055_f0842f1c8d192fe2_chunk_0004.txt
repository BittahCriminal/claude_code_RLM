Agreeing on a Definition of Platform Engineering
The term "platform engineering" has emerged as a pivotal concept in software engineering. However, despite its growing importance, there remains a significant lack of clarity and consensus around its precise definition and scope. This ambiguity can impede the adoption and effective implementation of platform engineering practices across organizations. Therefore, establishing a common understanding of platform engineering over the next one to two years is crucial for several reasons.
Platform engineering can be broadly defined as the practice of designing, building, and maintaining an
internal developer platform
(
IDP
) that serves as a cohesive and integrated set of tools, processes, and services. The primary goal of platform engineering is to enhance the efficiency, productivity, and satisfaction of development teams by providing a seamless and automated environment for software development and deployment.
The lack of a standardized definition of platform engineering leads to inconsistencies in how it is understood and implemented across different organizations. Some companies may focus primarily on infrastructure automation, while others might emphasize developer experience or CI/CD pipelines. This variability can result in fragmented and suboptimal implementations that fail to deliver the full potential of platform engineering.
Moreover, the rapid evolution of the cloud-native ecosystem has introduced a plethora of new tools, frameworks, and methodologies, each with its own set of capabilities and complexities. Organizations often struggle to navigate this landscape, piecing together various components without a clear strategy or roadmap. This can lead to inefficient use of resources, increased operational overhead, and slower time-to-market.
So, establishing a common understanding of platform engineering will provide several key benefits. Some are mentioned here:
Accelerated Adoption
: A clear and standardized definition will help organizations better understand the scope and objectives of platform engineering. This clarity will reduce hesitation and uncertainty, enabling companies to adopt platform engineering practices more quickly and confidently.
Improved Collaboration
: With a unified definition, teams will have a common language and framework to discuss their platform engineering efforts, share insights, and learn from each other's experiences. This collaborative environment can accelerate innovation and drive the development of more robust and effective platforms.
Enhanced Training and Development
: A standardized definition will facilitate the development and dissemination of training programs and educational resources. This will help build a skilled workforce capable of implementing and managing platform engineering practices, further accelerating adoption and improving outcomes.
Better Benchmarking and Best Practices
: A common understanding will enable organizations to benchmark their platform engineering efforts against industry standards and best practices. This can help identify areas for improvement, optimize resource allocation, and drive continuous improvement.
The community agreeing on a common understanding of Platform Engineering will play a crucial role for this field's future. As we agree on what we mean with the term, the team charter swill evolve for Platform team as well.
How Platform Engineering Teams will evolve
Platform engineering is increasingly becoming a vital aspect of modern software development and IT operations. However, many organizations still struggle with fragmented efforts, where platform engineering initiatives are spread across different departments, leading to inefficiencies and silos. As the field continues to mature, we anticipate significant changes in how platform engineering teams are structured and operate. This evolution will focus on breaking down silos, integrating efforts across departments, and making platforms more intuitive and seamless for end users.Currently, many organizations face the challenge of fragmented platform engineering efforts. Different teams and departments often work in isolation, each developing their own tools, processes, and platforms. This siloed approach can lead to several issues:
Duplication of Efforts
: Multiple teams may end up working on similar projects without knowledge of each other's work, leading to redundant efforts and wasted resources.
Inconsistent Standards
: Without a unified approach, different teams may adopt varying standards and practices, resulting in inconsistencies and interoperability issues.
Communication Barriers
: Siloed teams often face communication challenges, hindering collaboration and the sharing of knowledge and best practices.
Fragmented User Experience
: End users, typically developers, may have to navigate different platforms and tools, leading to a fragmented and inefficient experience.
One of the promises of Platform Engineering is to break down silos and improve collaboration using a common set of tools and processes. This is something in most cases that hasn't yet been a reality.
Building Platforms Hasn't Been Breaking Silos
To address the challenges of fragmented platform engineering, organizations need to shift their focus from merely building platforms to breaking down silos and fostering a more integrated approach. This involves several key strategies:
Unified Platform Strategy
: Develop a cohesive platform strategy that aligns with the organization's overall goals and priorities. This strategy should encompass all platform engineering efforts across departments, ensuring a unified direction and vision.
Cross-Functional Teams
: Establish cross-functional teams that bring together experts from different departments to collaborate on platform engineering projects. These teams can leverage diverse skills and perspectives, leading to more innovative and effective solutions.
Centralized Governance
: Implement centralized governance structures to oversee platform engineering efforts. This includes defining standards, best practices, and guidelines to ensure consistency and interoperability across different teams and platforms.
Effective Communication
: Foster open and transparent communication channels to facilitate collaboration and knowledge sharing. Regular meetings, workshops, and collaborative tools can help bridge communication gaps and promote a culture of teamwork.
Integrated Tools and Processes
: Adopt integrated tools and processes that support seamless collaboration and coordination across teams. This includes unified CI/CD pipelines, shared code repositories, and common monitoring and observability tools.
One of the key goals of platform engineering is to make platforms "invisible" to end users. This means creating platforms that are so intuitive and seamless that developers can focus on their core tasks without being bogged down by the underlying infrastructure and processes. Achieving this requires several important steps:
User-Centric Design
: Design platforms with a strong focus on the user experience. Understand the needs and pain points of developers and create solutions that simplify their workflows and enhance productivity.
Self-Service Capabilities
: Provide robust self-service capabilities that allow developers to access and utilize platform resources without needing to go through complex approval processes or relying on other teams. This includes self-service portals, automated provisioning, and easy-to-use APIs.
Automation and Abstraction
: Automate repetitive tasks and abstract away the complexities of the underlying infrastructure. This allows developers to focus on writing code and delivering features, rather than managing infrastructure and configurations.
Seamless Integration
: Ensure that platforms integrate seamlessly with the tools and workflows that developers are already using. This includes integration with popular IDEs, version control systems, CI/CD pipelines, and monitoring tools.
Continuous Improvement
: Continuously gather feedback from developers and iterate on the platform to address their evolving needs and challenges. This involves regularly updating and enhancing the platform based on user feedback and technological advancements.
Documentation
: Provide great documentation and guides of the services the platform provides. This can come in the form of documentation, blueprints, even service catalogs.
As organizations adopt these strategies, platform engineering teams will evolve in several important ways:
Greater Collaboration
: Cross-functional collaboration will become the norm, with teams working together to build and maintain integrated platforms that serve the entire organization.
Enhanced Efficiency
: By breaking down silos and adopting unified strategies, organizations will achieve greater efficiency in their platform engineering efforts, reducing duplication and optimizing resource utilization.
Improved Developer Experience
: Platforms will become more user-friendly and intuitive, enabling developers to focus on their core tasks and improving overall productivity and satisfaction.
Scalability and Flexibility

Innovation and Agility
: By fostering a culture of collaboration and continuous improvement, organizations will drive innovation and agility, staying ahead of the competition in a rapidly evolving technological landscape.
The evolution of platform engineering teams will be marked by a shift from fragmented, siloed efforts to integrated, collaborative approaches that prioritize user experience and efficiency. Is Kubernetes the endgame?
Kubernetes has become a cornerstone of modern cloud-native architecture, providing a powerful, flexible, and scalable platform for managing containerized applications. However, as the ecosystem evolves, questions arise about whether Kubernetes is the ultimate solution or if it will give way to new paradigms and tools. This section explores the complexities and potentials of Kubernetes, its role as an API-based workload scheduler, the growing necessity for multi-cluster deployments, and the future of Kubernetes in platform engineering.Kubernetes, while powerful, is often perceived as overly complex, especially by newcomers. This perception can be attributed to its vast array of features, the steep learning curve associated with mastering its components, and the challenges of managing large-scale deployments. However, much of this complexity can be mitigated through proper education, tooling, and abstraction layers.Kubernetes abstracts the underlying infrastructure, providing a consistent and declarative approach to deploying and managing applications. This abstraction is powerful but can be daunting without a clear understanding of its principles and best practices. To address this, many organizations are investing in training and creating simplified interfaces that hide much of the underlying complexity.Efforts such as the Kubernetes learning resources, and community-driven tutorials have made it easier for developers and operators to get started with Kubernetes. Moreover, managed Kubernetes services from cloud providers like
Google Kubernetes Engine
(
GKE
), Amazon's
Elastic Kubernetes Service
(
EKS
), and
Azure Kubernetes Service
(
AKS
) further simplify the operational aspects, allowing teams to focus more on application development and less on infrastructure management.At its core, Kubernetes is nothing more than a declarative API that helps us declaratively describe and manage our workloads. It provides a powerful API that allows developers and operators to declaratively define the desired state of their applications and infrastructure. Kubernetes then takes on the responsibility of ensuring that the actual state matches the desired state, handling tasks such as scaling, healing, and updating applications.The power of Kubernetes lies in its extensibility and flexibility. It is designed to be modular, allowing organizations to extend its capabilities through custom controllers, operators, and Custom Resource Definitions (CRDs). This extensibility enables teams to tailor Kubernetes to their specific needs, integrating it seamlessly with their existing tools and workflows.Despite its perceived complexity, many of the challenges associated with Kubernetes are gradually being addressed. For example, the Kubernetes community has been working on improving the user experience by simplifying the API, enhancing documentation, and developing more intuitive tooling. As a result, many of the complexities that once made Kubernetes daunting are being mitigated, making it more accessible and user-friendly.The recent (in v1.29) removal of over a million lines of Cloud Provider code from Kubernetes is a significant step towards simplifying the platform. This effort, part of the
Kubernetes Enhancement Proposal
(
KEP
) 2395, aims to move vendor-specific code out-of-tree, reducing the out-of-box complexity and making Kubernetes more modular and easier to manage. These changes reflect the ongoing efforts to streamline Kubernetes and make it more adaptable to various use cases.While there is an effort to simplify Kubernetes at its core, there is also the need for managing complex deployments and set ups. As we see companies trying to rationalize their cloud spend and adopt hybrid cloud strategies, the need for multi-cluster, hybrid deployments become very important.
Multi-cluster is becoming a necessity
As organizations increasingly adopt Kubernetes, the need for multi-cluster deployments has become more apparent. Multi-cluster setups provide several advantages, including improved fault tolerance, better resource utilization, and enhanced security through isolation. They also support hybrid and multi-cloud strategies, allowing organizations to deploy applications across different cloud providers and on-premises environments.Managing multiple Kubernetes clusters, however, introduces new challenges. These include the complexity of managing cluster configurations, ensuring consistent security policies, and coordinating deployments across clusters. Tools and frameworks such as Kubernetes Federation, Cluster API, and open-source projects like Rancher and KubeFed are emerging to address these challenges, providing centralized management and orchestration for multi-cluster environments.A notable development in this space is the trend towards centralized add-on management for multi-cluster Kubernetes environments. This approach simplifies the deployment and management of common services, such as monitoring, logging, and security, across multiple clusters. It allows organizations to maintain consistency and streamline operations, making multi-cluster Kubernetes more practical and efficient.Moreover, the increasing adoption of edge computing and IoT is driving the need for multi-cluster deployments. These environments often require applications to be deployed close to the data source or end-users, necessitating distributed and geographically dispersed clusters. Kubernetes, with its robust scheduling and orchestration capabilities, is well-suited to manage these complex, distributed environments.
Kubernetes was not designed to build Platforms
Kubernetes was originally designed to be a container orchestration platform, focusing on scheduling and managing containerized workloads. It provides a robust framework for deploying, scaling, and managing applications in a distributed environment. However, as the needs of the cloud-native ecosystem have evolved, Kubernetes has been extended to support a broader range of use cases, including platform engineering.The core strength of Kubernetes lies in its ability to abstract the underlying infrastructure and provide a consistent interface for managing workloads. This makes it an ideal foundation for building platforms that can support diverse applications and services. However, Kubernetes was not initially designed with platform engineering in mind, and this has led to certain limitations and challenges.One of the key challenges is the complexity of integrating various tools and services into a cohesive platform. While Kubernetes provides the foundational infrastructure, building a complete platform requires additional components for monitoring, logging, security, CI/CD, and more. This often involves stitching together multiple open-source projects and custom solutions, which can be cumbersome and difficult to manage.To address these challenges, the Kubernetes community and the broader cloud-native ecosystem are exploring ways to simplify and streamline platform engineering. This includes the development of opinionated distributions and frameworks that provide integrated solutions out of the box.Looking ahead, it is likely that we will see further evolution and specialization within the Kubernetes ecosystem. This could include the development of forks or specialized distributions that focus specifically on platform engineering needs. These specialized versions of Kubernetes would aim to provide the robust scheduling and orchestration capabilities of Kubernetes while abstracting away much of the complexity and integrating essential platform services.As Kubernetes evolves, we do expect a significant evolution in the whole cloud-native ecosystem.
The evolution of cloud-native tools
The evolution of cloud-native tools is inevitable as the ecosystem matures. Currently, the landscape is vast and complex, with numerous tools and frameworks emerging to address various aspects of cloud-native development and operations. This diversity, while beneficial in fostering innovation, also poses significant challenges. Many organizations struggle to navigate the plethora of available tools, leading to fragmentation and inefficiencies. Over the coming years, we can expect to see significant consolidation and evolution in this space, aimed at simplifying the landscape and providing more comprehensive and user-friendly solutions.The cloud-native ecosystem, largely driven by the
Cloud Native Computing Foundation
(
CNCF
), has grown exponentially over the past few years. With over 100 projects under its umbrella, CNCF provides a rich repository of tools for container orchestration, service meshes, observability, CI/CD, security, and more. While this abundance of tools offers flexibility, it also creates complexity. Organizations often find themselves overwhelmed by the choices and the effort required to integrate these disparate tools into a cohesive platform.For instance, consider the domain of observability. Tools like Prometheus, Grafana, Jaeger, and Loki each provide valuable capabilities, but integrating them into a unified observability stack can be challenging. Similar complexities arise in other areas such as security, CI/CD pipelines, and service meshes.This fragmented approach with multiple solutions addressing problems in the same space can lead to inefficiencies, increased operational overhead, and a steep learning curve for teams.
The need for ready-to-use enterprise platforms
Despite the plethora of tools available, there is still no comprehensive, ready-to-use enterprise platform that addresses all aspects of cloud-native development and operations. Most existing solutions either focus on specific areas (like developer portals or platform orchestrators) or require significant effort to integrate and customize.For instance, platforms like OpenShift provide a robust enterprise-grade Kubernetes platform with integrated tools for CI/CD, monitoring, and security. However, OpenShift is known for being opinionated and difficult to customize, which can be a limitation for organizations with specific needs. While it offers strong defaults, the flexibility to tailor the platform to unique requirements is limited.This gap presents an opportunity for new solutions that offer strong defaults while being flexible enough to accommodate customizations. Such platforms would need to provide a seamless, integrated experience that covers all aspects of the cloud-native lifecycle, from development and testing to deployment, monitoring, and scaling.
The Future: Strong Defaults with Flexible Options
As the cloud-native ecosystem continues to evolve, we can expect to see the emergence of platforms that strike a balance between strong defaults and flexibility. These platforms will aim to reduce complexity by providing integrated solutions out of the box while allowing for customization to meet specific needs.
Integrated Solutions
: Future platforms will bundle essential tools and services into a cohesive package, reducing the effort required to integrate and manage them. These platforms will offer pre-configured environments that follow best practices, making it easier for organizations to get started and achieve quick wins.
Flexibility and Customization
: While strong defaults are essential for reducing complexity, flexibility will be crucial for addressing unique requirements. Future platforms will offer modular architectures that allow organizations to swap out components or add new ones without disrupting the overall system. This will enable teams to tailor the platform to their specific needs while maintaining a seamless user experience.
Standardization and Interoperability
: Efforts to standardize APIs and interfaces will facilitate interoperability between different tools and platforms. This will make it easier for organizations to integrate new tools, switch between solutions, and avoid vendor lock-in. Standards like the Open Application Model (OAM) and the Service Mesh Interface (SMI) are steps in this direction.
Enhanced User Experience
: Future platforms will prioritize user experience, making it easier for developers and operators to interact with the platform. This will involve intuitive user interfaces, comprehensive documentation, and robust support for automation and self-service capabilities.
Currently, most platforms are built on top of CNCF tools, leveraging the rich ecosystem of open-source projects. However, we can expect to see more enterprise solutions emerging that package and distribute these tools in a more user-friendly and integrated manner. Some of those are mentioned here:
Packaging and Distribution
: Enterprises will look for solutions that package CNCF tools with enterprise-grade features such as enhanced security, compliance, and support. This will make it easier for organizations to adopt these tools without the need for extensive customization and integration efforts.
Enterprise-Grade Features
: Future platforms will incorporate features that address enterprise requirements, such as
role-based access control
(
RBAC
), auditing, compliance, and scalability. These features will be built into the platform, reducing the need for organizations to implement them separately.
Support and Maintenance
: Enterprises will seek platforms that come with robust support and maintenance services, ensuring that they can rely on the platform for mission-critical applications. This will include regular updates, security patches, and access to expert support.
In conclusion, the cloud-native tools landscape is poised for significant evolution and consolidation. The current complexity and redundancy present challenges, but they also offer opportunities for innovation. Future platforms will aim to provide strong defaults with flexible options, integrating essential tools into cohesive packages that simplify cloud-native development and operations.As these platforms evolve, they will prioritize user experience, standardization, and enterprise-grade features, making it easier for organizations to adopt and benefit from cloud-native technologies. By striking a balance between simplicity and flexibility, these platforms will enable organizations to build robust, scalable, and efficient cloud-native environments that drive innovation and business value.This consolidation and evolution will ultimately lead to a more streamlined and user-friendly cloud-native ecosystem, reducing the barriers to adoption and enabling organizations to fully leverage the benefits of cloud-native technologies.
