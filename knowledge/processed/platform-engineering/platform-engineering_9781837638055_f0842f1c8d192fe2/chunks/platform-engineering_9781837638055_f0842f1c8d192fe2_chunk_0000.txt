Technical infrastructure before the public cloud and DevOps
Technical infrastructure
refers to the collection of tools that support the operation and management of an organization's information systems, including—but not limited to—hardware, software, and network resources. It includes physical infrastructure—such as servers, storage devices, and networking equipment—as well as virtual infrastructure—such as virtual servers and virtual storage. Technical infrastructure also includes software components, such as the operating systems, applications, and tools that are used to manage and maintain the hardware and software resources. Technical infrastructure is a critical component of an organization's IT environment as it enables the delivery of services and applications to users and supports the storage and processing of data. It plays a key role in enabling organizations to achieve their business objectives by providing the foundation for information systems and the underlying technology infrastructure. In the software world, technical infrastructure is the collection of tools and applications needed to build, deliver, and maintain software.
Technical infrastructure can be managed in-house, where the organization is responsible for the design, implementation, and maintenance of the infrastructure, or it can be outsourced to a third-party provider. Outsourcing technical infrastructure can provide organizations with access to expertise and resources that they may not have in-house, as well as cost savings through economies of scale. However, outsourcing also carries the risk of vendor lock-in and can be more difficult to customize and adapt to changing business needs.
Evolution of technical infrastructure
The concept of technical infrastructure has evolved over time as the needs and capabilities of organizations have changed. In the early days of computing, technical infrastructure was limited to a few mainframe computers that were used to support a limited number of business functions. These computers were expensive and required specialized skills to operate and maintain, and as a result, they were typically managed in-house by a dedicated team of IT professionals. As computers became more powerful and affordable, organizations began to adopt decentralized computing models, with multiple servers and workstations that were connected through
local area networks
(
LANs
). This decentralized model made it possible for organizations to support a wider range of business functions and enabled users to access data and applications from any location within the organization. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, the rise of the internet and the emergence of web-based applications led to a shift toward more distributed and decentralized technical infrastructure. Web servers and application servers were added to the mix, and networks were expanded to support the growing number of devices and users. This shift toward distributed computing required organizations to adopt new tools and processes for managing and maintaining their technical infrastructure, as well as new approaches to security and data management. In the past couple of decades, the emergence of cloud computing has revolutionized the way organizations manage their technical infrastructure. With the advent of public cloud providers, such as
Amazon Web Services
(
AWS
), Microsoft Azure, and
Google Cloud Platform
(
GCP
), organizations can now
outsource
the management of their hardware, software, and networking resources to a third-party provider. This has made it easier for organizations to scale their technical infrastructure and has enabled them to access a wide range of services and resources on demand, without the need to invest in expensive hardware and software.
Issues with traditional technical infrastructure management
Before the advent of cloud computing and the rise of the DevOps movement, the operations and administration of technical infrastructure were typically labor-intensive processes that involved a variety of manual tasks and processes. These tasks included installing and configuring hardware and software, monitoring and maintaining systems, and troubleshooting issues as they arose. Operations and administration of traditional technical infrastructure was typically the responsibility of a dedicated IT team, which was responsible for managing the hardware, software, and networking resources that supported the organization's information systems. This team would work closely with other teams, such as the development team, to ensure that systems were designed, implemented, and maintained in a way that met the needs of the organization.
Traditional infrastructure management posed several challenges and limitations for organizations, as outlined here:
High maintenance and uptime requirements
: To maintain a high level of availability and uptime, IT teams had to be on call 24/7 to respond to issues and perform regular maintenance and updates. This often led to IT professionals working long hours and being available to troubleshoot issues at any time of the day or night.
Difficulty in scaling
: Scaling infrastructure to meet changing business needs required significant investment in hardware and software, as well as time and effort to install and configure the new resources. This could be a slow and cumbersome process, making it difficult for organizations to keep pace with changing business needs.
Long lead times for making changes
: Manual processes and documentation and the need to follow a structured change management process made it challenging to implement new systems or make changes to existing ones quickly. This could take weeks or even months, making it difficult for organizations to keep up with changing business needs.
Inflexibility
: The inflexibility of traditional systems and processes made it challenging for organizations to respond quickly to new market conditions or take advantage of new opportunities.
Poor visibility into system status
: Lack of real-time monitoring and reliance on manual processes for gathering information about the status of systems made it challenging for IT professionals to troubleshoot issues quickly.
There were several best practices that organizations used to follow in order to manage technical infrastructure before the rise of DevOps. Organizations would maintain detailed documentation of their technical infrastructure, including diagrams of the hardware and software components, as well as instructions for installation and configuration. This documentation was critical for troubleshooting issues and for maintaining the systems over time. Additionally, they would use a variety of tools to monitor the performance and availability of their systems, including system monitoring tools, application performance monitoring tools, and network monitoring tools. These tools would provide alerts when issues arose, and IT professionals could use them to troubleshoot problems and identify the root cause of issues. A very well-structured process for managing changes to their technical infrastructure would be followed, including procedures for testing, staging, and rolling out updates and new releases. This process was designed to minimize the risk of disruption and to ensure that changes were made in a controlled and predictable manner. Organizations would maintain comprehensive backup and recovery procedures to ensure that their systems and data were protected in the event of an outage or a disaster. These procedures would include regular backups of data and systems, as well as processes for restoring systems in the event of an issue. Finally, there was a need to implement a variety of security measures to protect their systems and data from external threats, including firewalls,
intrusion detection systems
(
IDS
), and antivirus software. They would also follow best practices for password management and user access control to ensure that systems were secure and compliant.
The emergence of DevOps and cloud computing
While these best practices helped organizations manage their technical infrastructure effectively, they also had some limitations. Reliance on manual processes and documentation made it difficult to adapt to changing business needs and respond quickly to issues as they arose. The emergence of the
DevOps
movement and the adoption of cloud computing has helped organizations overcome these limitations and improve the speed and reliability of their IT operations. We'll explore this more in the next parts of this chapter. Managing traditional technical infrastructure posed a number of challenges for organizations, particularly in terms of maintenance, scalability, and agility. The DevOps movement promotes a more agile and flexible approach to infrastructure management, with an emphasis on automation, CD and continuous improvement.
DevOps is a set of practices that promotes collaboration and integration between development and operations teams, with the goal of improving the efficiency and effectiveness of software delivery. The DevOps movement has its roots in the agile software development movement, which emerged in the late 1990s and early 2000s. Agile software development emphasized the importance of rapid iteration, continuous delivery, and customer feedback in the software development process. The DevOps movement took this idea a step further by promoting the integration of development and operations teams and by emphasizing the importance of automation and continuous improvement in the software delivery process.

It has also played a key role in the transition to cloud computing and the adoption of more agile and flexible approaches to IT operations. The DevOps movement emerged as a response to the challenges of traditional infrastructure management, particularly in terms of the difficulty of managing software in on-premises models. The rise of DevOps and cloud computing are closely linked, as both are part of a broader shift toward more agile and flexible approaches to IT operations. Cloud computing provides a platform for organizations to host and run their software applications and services in a scalable and cost-effective manner. It also allows for rapid provisioning and deprovisioning of resources, which is a key enabler of DevOps principles such as continuous delivery and automation. The traditional approach to infrastructure management involved managing hardware and software in on-premises models, which were often slow, cumbersome, and expensive. This made it difficult to rapidly provision and deprovision resources and to make changes to the infrastructure the DevOps way.
The rise of cloud computing has transformed this approach, providing organizations with a more flexible and scalable model for managing their IT operations. With cloud computing, organizations can provision and deprovision resources quickly and easily and can scale their infrastructure up or down in response to changing business needs. This has made it possible to implement DevOps principles such as continuous delivery, which involves the frequent and rapid release of new software updates and features. Cloud computing has also provided the tools and infrastructure needed to support automation and collaboration between development and operations teams, which are key components of the DevOps philosophy. By enabling teams to work together more closely and to automate routine tasks, cloud computing has helped to streamline the software delivery process and to improve the speed and reliability of IT operations.
The shift toward cloud infrastructure
Cloud computing has revolutionized the way organizations manage their IT infrastructure, and it has become an essential part of the modern IT landscape. Benefits and hurdles in the adoption of cloud technologies
The use of public cloud services has a number of benefits for organizations. Public cloud services are generally more cost-effective than on-premises solutions, as they allow organizations to pay only for the resources they consume and to scale up or down as needed.This can help organizations to reduce their IT costs and to better align their IT spend with their business needs. This can help organizations to respond more quickly to changing business needs and to support rapid growth.Public cloud providers invest heavily in security and offer a range of security measures and controls to protect their customers' data. This can help organizations to reduce their risk of data breaches and to comply with various security and regulatory requirements. Organizations can stay competitive and innovative gaining access to the latest technologies through public cloud providers, as they continuously invest to bring the latest technologies into the cloud. While public cloud providers offer robust security measures and controls, organizations are still responsible for the security of their own data and applications. This means that organizations should implement appropriate security measures and controls, such as encryption and access controls, to protect their data in the cloud. Public cloud services may require organizations to make changes to their existing IT infrastructure and processes, which can be challenging. Organizations should carefully evaluate the impact of moving to the cloud and ensure that they have the necessary resources and expertise to manage the transition. The relationship between cloud computing and DevOps
The transition to cloud infrastructure has been a key driver of the DevOps movement, as it has enabled organizations to become more agile and flexible in the way they manage their IT infrastructure. DevOps engineering plays a critical role in this transition, as it involves the design and management of tools and processes that support the delivery and management of applications at scale. Additionally, DevOps engineers play a crucial role in transitioning to cloud infrastructure. Some of their key responsibilities include designing and implementing automation tools and processes, managing and monitoring cloud environments, collaborating with development and operations teams, implementing security measures, managing vendor relationships, and providing technical guidance and support.One of the primary responsibilities of DevOps engineers is designing and implementing automation tools and processes that streamline the development, testing, and deployment of applications in the cloud. This includes tools for CI/CD, configuration management, and
Infrastructure as Code
(

). DevOps engineers are also responsible for managing and monitoring cloud environments, ensuring that applications are performing optimally, and identifying and resolving issues as they arise. They collaborate with development and operations teams to design and implement applications effectively and develop tools and processes to support their delivery and management.Another critical responsibility of DevOps engineers is implementing security measures to protect data in the cloud. This includes using encryption, access controls, and security monitoring to safeguard data from unauthorized access or theft. DevOps engineers may also be responsible for managing vendor relationships with cloud providers, negotiating contracts and

Defining DevOps maturity
There are different levels of DevOps maturity that organizations can progress through as they adopt DevOps practices. At the lowest level, organizations may have a basic understanding of DevOps principles and may have implemented some basic automation processes. As they progress through the different levels of maturity, organizations may implement more advanced automation processes and adopt a more robust culture of collaboration and continuous improvement.One way to understand the progress and effectiveness of an organization's DevOps practices is to use a DevOps maturity model. A DevOps maturity model is a framework that helps organizations to assess their current level of DevOps maturity and to identify areas for improvement. There are many different DevOps maturity models available, but most of them generally include the following levels:
Level 1: Siloed
: At this level, development and operations teams are largely separate and operate in silos, with little collaboration or coordination between them. There may be limited automation and few processes in place to support the delivery and management of applications.
Level 2: Coordinated
: At this level, development and operations teams begin to coordinate their efforts and work more closely together. There may be some automation in place, but processes are often ad hoc and inconsistent.
Level 3: Integrated
: At this level, development and operations teams are fully integrated and work collaboratively to deliver and manage applications. There is a greater focus on automation and on standardizing processes to support the delivery and management of applications.
Level 4: Continuous
: At this level, development and operations teams have fully embraced DevOps principles and practices and are able to continuously deliver and manage applications. There is a strong focus on automation, continuous improvement, and continuous learning, and processes are fully integrated and optimized.

Level 2: Managed
: At this level, organizations have some automation in place and have established basic processes to support the delivery and management of applications.
Level 3: Defined
: At this level, organizations have fully defined and standardized their DevOps processes and have a strong focus on automation.
Level 4: Measured
: At this level, organizations have fully integrated their DevOps processes and are able to continuously monitor and optimize them.
Level 5: Optimized
: At this level, organizations have fully optimized their DevOps processes and are able to continuously learn and improve.

Architectural complexity of SaaS
There are several SaaS architectures in use today, each with their own unique challenges and considerations. Let’s look at a few options.
Single vs. multi-tenant
A common architectural decision SaaS providers have to make today is choosing between single or multi-tenant architectures.
Single-tenant architectures
involve each customer having their own dedicated instance of the software application, while multi-tenant architectures involve multiple customers sharing the same instance. Single-tenant architectures provide greater control and customization options for customers but can be more complex to manage and scale.
Multi-tenant architectures
, on the other hand, offer greater scalability and efficiency but can be more challenging to customize and secure.
Deciding between single or multi-tenant architectures has a major implication on how an application is deployed and scaled as shown in the following screenshot:
Figure 2.1- Deployment of single vs multi-tenant application
Other challenges associated with single and multi-tenant architectures include:
Managing data isolation and security
: In single-tenant architectures, data is isolated per customer, which can make it easier to manage security and compliance requirements. However, this also means that there are multiple instances of the application to manage and secure. In multi-tenant architectures, backends and databases are shared between customers, which can make it more challenging to ensure data isolation and protect against data breaches.
Providing customizations and upgrades for individual customers
: Single-tenant architectures provide greater flexibility for customizations and upgrades, as each customer has their own dedicated instance. However, this can also lead to a more complex release management process, as each customer's instance needs to be upgraded separately. In multi-tenant architectures, customizations and upgrades need to be applied across all customers, which can be more challenging to manage.
Ensuring scalability and performance across multiple customers and instances
: Multi-tenant architectures provide greater scalability and efficiency, as multiple customers can be served from the same instance. However, this also means that performance and scalability issues can affect multiple customers, which can have a significant impact on the user experience. In single-tenant architectures, each customer has their own dedicated instance, which can make it easier to isolate performance and scalability issues.
Microservices
Microservices architectures involve breaking down software applications into smaller, independent components that can be developed and deployed separately. Containerization technologies such as Docker and Kubernetes enable the packaging and deployment of microservices in a scalable and efficient way. This also has a significant impact on the complexity of a system as shown below.
Figure 2.2- Monolithic vs Microservice architecture
Challenges associated with microservices, and containerization include:
Managing the complexity of distributed systems
: Microservices architectures can be more complex to manage than monolithic architectures, as there are multiple components to coordinate and manage. Containerization adds an additional layer of complexity, as containers need to be orchestrated and managed across multiple hosts and environments.
Ensuring reliability and availability across multiple services and instances
: In microservices architectures, a failure in one service can affect the availability and reliability of other services. This can make it more challenging to ensure high availability and reliability across the entire application. Containerization can help mitigate this by enabling automatic failover and scaling of containers.
Managing orchestration and coordination between services and containers
: Microservices architectures require effective orchestration and coordination between services and containers. This includes managing service discovery, load balancing, and communication between services and containers
Cloud-native
Cloud-native technologies such as serverless computing, functions-as-a-service, and event-driven architectures provide new ways of building and delivering software applications as services. These technologies enable greater scalability, agility, and efficiency, but also introduce new challenges.Challenges associated with cloud-native technologies include:
Managing statelessness and data persistence in serverless environments
: Serverless computing environments are designed to be stateless, which can make it challenging to manage data persistence and ensure consistency across multiple invocations. This requires careful consideration of data management and persistence strategies.
Ensuring security and compliance in distributed systems
: Cloud-native architectures are often distributed across multiple services and environments, which can make it more challenging to ensure security and compliance. This requires effective access control, data encryption, and monitoring across all services and environments.
Managing the complexity of event-driven architectures and event processing
: Event-driven architectures are designed to be loosely coupled and event-driven, which can make them more scalable and flexible. However, this also adds complexity to the application, as events need to be processed and coordinated across multiple services and environments. Effective event processing and coordination is essential to ensure the reliability and performance of the application.
SaaS providers face a range of challenges when designing, building, and delivering software applications as services. These challenges span multiple areas, including scalability, security, performance, and compliance.
Scalability
is a key challenge for SaaS providers, as they need to be able to accommodate a rapidly growing number of users and customers while maintaining high levels of performance and availability. Scalability challenges can arise at multiple levels, including infrastructure, application design, and database architecture. Infrastructure scalability requires the ability to scale up or down compute and storage resources quickly and efficiently. This can be achieved through cloud-based infrastructure and services, such as auto-scaling groups, load balancers, and managed databases. Application scalability requires designing applications that can handle a large number of users and requests in a scalable and efficient way. This can be achieved through techniques such as horizontal scaling, caching, and sharding. Database scalability requires designing databases that can handle a large amount of data and requests in a scalable and efficient way. This can be achieved through techniques such as database partitioning, replication, and caching.
Security
is another key challenge for SaaS providers, as they need to ensure that their applications and data are protected from unauthorized access, data breaches, and other security threats. Security challenges can arise at multiple levels, including infrastructure, application design, and data management. Infrastructure security requires ensuring that cloud-based infrastructure and services are secure and compliant with relevant standards and regulations. This includes implementing access control, data encryption, and monitoring across all services and environments. Application security requires designing applications that are secure by design, with effective access control, authentication, and authorization mechanisms in place. Data management security requires ensuring that data is secure and compliant with relevant standards and regulations. This includes implementing data encryption, access control, and monitoring across all data stores and environments.
Performance
is a critical challenge for SaaS providers, as users expect fast and responsive applications that can handle large amounts of data and requests. Performance challenges can arise at multiple levels, including infrastructure, application design, and database architecture. Performance requires ensuring that cloud-based infrastructure and services are optimized for performance, with low latency, high throughput, and fast response times. This includes optimizing network and storage performance, as well as selecting appropriate instance types and configurations. Application performance requires designing applications that are optimized for performance, with efficient algorithms, caching, and other techniques in place. Database performance requires designing databases that are optimized for performance, with appropriate indexing, partitioning, and caching strategies in place.
Compliance
is a critical challenge for SaaS providers, as they need to ensure that their applications and data are compliant with relevant laws, regulations, and standards. Compliance challenges can arise at multiple levels, including infrastructure, application design, and data management. Infrastructure compliance includes implementing data encryption, access control, and monitoring across all services and environments, as well as ensuring compliance with relevant certifications such as SOC 2, PCI DSS, and HIPAA.Application compliance requires designing applications that are compliant with relevant laws, regulations, and standards, with effective data privacy, data protection, and data retention policies in place. Data management compliance requires ensuring that data is compliant with relevant laws, regulations, and standards, including data privacy and data protection regulations such as GDPR and CCPA. This includes implementing appropriate data encryption, access control, and monitoring across all data stores and environments, as well as ensuring compliance with relevant data protection and retention policies.

Defining platform boundaries
When building SaaS products, it is important to understand the distinction between platform and product. The platform provides the foundation on which multiple products can be built, while products are the specific applications or services that are delivered to customers. In this section, we will explore the relationship between platform and product, platform-product boundaries and dependencies, separation of concerns, and API-driven integration of platform and product
Platform and product
The relationship between platform and product is symbiotic. The platform provides the underlying infrastructure and services that products can leverage to deliver value to customers, while the products themselves drive the need for new features and capabilities in the platform. The platform is like the engine of a car, while the product is like the body. The engine is what powers the car, but the body is what people see and interact with.For example, a company may build a platform that provides authentication, billing, metering, and customer onboarding services. Products built on this platform can leverage these services to quickly and easily implement these features without having to build them from scratch. As products evolve and new features are needed, the platform can be extended to provide additional services and functionality.
Platform-product boundaries and dependencies
To ensure the success of both the platform and products, it is important to define clear boundaries and dependencies between the two. The platform should provide a standardized set of services and APIs that products can use to implement their specific functionality. At the same time, the platform should not be tightly coupled to any specific product, as this can limit the flexibility and scalability of the platform.Products should also be designed to minimize their dependencies on the platform. While products may rely on certain platform services, they should be able to function independently if those services are not available. This allows products to be built and tested independently of the platform, which can help speed up development and reduce complexity.
Separation of concerns
One of the key principles in platform engineering is the separation of concerns between the platform and product teams. The platform team is responsible for creating and maintaining the tools and services that the product teams use to build and deploy their applications. The product teams, on the other hand, are responsible for using these tools and services to create the applications that provide value to customers.By separating these responsibilities, the platform team can focus on providing a high-quality, scalable, and secure set of tools and services that can be used by all product teams, while the product teams can focus on creating the applications that differentiate the company and provide value to customers.This separation of concerns also allows the platform team to make changes and updates to the platform without disrupting the work of the product teams. By using APIs and other integration points, the platform team can provide new features and services to the product teams without requiring them to make significant changes to their applicationsAPI-driven integration is a key aspect of platform engineering. APIs allow the product teams to integrate their applications with the platform services without having to understand the underlying implementation details. This allows the product teams to focus on building their applications and providing value to customers, while the platform team can focus on providing a scalable, secure, and reliable set of services.API-driven integration also allows the platform team to make changes and updates to the platform services without requiring the product teams to make significant changes to their applications. Satisfaction

The

(
DORA
) metrics are a set of standardized indicators that measure the effectiveness of DevOps practices. By tracking these metrics, organizations can gain insights into their development and operational processes and identify areas for improvement. These metrics can also help organizations understand the impact of platform engineering efforts on their overall productivity and align their goals with industry best practices. The four DORA metrics include:
Deployment Frequency
: This metric reflects the number of times code is deployed to production within a specific period. Higher deployment frequencies can indicate increased productivity, as teams are able to deliver new features and updates more rapidly.
Lead Time for Changes
: Lead time measures the duration from the moment a code change is committed to when it is successfully deployed in production. Shorter lead times are indicative of a more efficient development process, which can lead to higher productivity.
Time to Restore Service
: This metric measures the time it takes to restore a service to normal operation after a failure or incident. Faster restoration times can suggest that teams are more adept at identifying and resolving issues, contributing to increased productivity.
Change Failure Rate
: Change failure rate represents the percentage of deployments that result in a failure, requiring rollback or hotfix. Lower change failure rates are indicative of a more stable and reliable development process, leading to higher productivity.

Pillar 3: Ensuring security and compliance
The adoption of cloud computing brings many benefits to organizations, such as increased scalability, cost savings, and reduced operational overhead. However, it also presents unique challenges related to security and compliance.In this section, we will discuss some of the key challenges associated with securing cloud environments and ensuring compliance with relevant regulations:
Shared responsibility model for cloud security
: One of the fundamental concepts in cloud security is the shared responsibility model, which outlines the security responsibilities of both the cloud provider and the customer. Cloud providers are responsible for securing the underlying infrastructure, while customers are responsible for securing the data and applications running on that infrastructure. Understanding and managing these shared responsibilities can be complex, and organizations must establish clear guidelines for the division of security tasks between their internal teams and cloud providers.
Compliance with industry-specific regulations
: Organizations operating in regulated industries, such as healthcare, finance, or government, must adhere to strict regulatory requirements regarding data protection and privacy. Compliance in the cloud can be challenging, as organizations must ensure that their cloud provider meets the necessary standards and that they have the right controls in place to maintain compliance.
Protecting sensitive data and maintaining privacy
: Cloud environments often store and process vast amounts of sensitive data, making them attractive targets for cybercriminals. Organizations must implement robust data protection measures, such as encryption and tokenization, to secure sensitive information both at rest and in transit. Additionally, organizations need to manage privacy concerns by ensuring that they have appropriate policies and procedures in place for handling personal information, as well as complying with data protection regulations like the
General Data Protection Regulation
(
GDPR
).
Managing access control and authentication
: In cloud environments, managing access to resources and applications becomes more complex due to the distributed nature of the infrastructure. Organizations must establish strong access control policies and implement
Multi-Factor Authentication
(
MFA
) to ensure that only authorized users can access sensitive resources. This also involves managing the permissions and privileges of different user roles, such as developers, administrators, and end-users, to minimize the risk of unauthorized access or accidental data exposure.
Ensuring continuous monitoring and threat detection
: Cloud environments are dynamic, with resources being provisioned and deprovisioned rapidly. This requires organizations to adopt a proactive approach to security monitoring and threat detection, as traditional perimeter-based security measures are often insufficient. By implementing continuous monitoring tools and leveraging cloud-native security services, organizations can detect and respond to threats more effectively, reducing the risk of security breaches and data loss.
